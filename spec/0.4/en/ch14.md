## 14 Layout intent

### 14.1 Common rules

- A constraint makes a constraint object. A named constraint can be referred to, which helps to find and edit it, such as `%row1 = {#M1 #M2} @ALIGN("y")`; a constraint can also be anonymous.
- **Vocabulary**: the atomic constraints on positions are only ALIGN, ORDER, DISTRIBUTE and DISTANCE, besides PLACE, ORIENT, GROUP and TABLE (14.11), which is orders written as a table. Other relations are written as general definitions: symmetry (14.7), centring (a DISTRIBUTE of three members), rows and columns (ALIGN with ORDER), signal flow (14.6), mirrored pairs, spacings in proportion (several exact DISTANCEs). There is no LEFT or ABOVE (they are an ORDER or a DISTANCE with a direction), and no RATIO, NEAR or INSIDE. Relative orientation (the same orientation, mirror images) is ORIENT-RELATION (14.9); whether the gates of a pair face each other or face outwards is still fixed with an ORIENT, or with an ORDER on terminals (14.3). That symbols do not overlap, spacing constants and grids belong to the solver (11.7) and are not part of the vocabulary of constraints.
- **No positions relative to the whole drawing**: there is no keyword for "leftmost", "rightmost" or "topmost", and no extent of the whole drawing or of symbol outlines. Write an ORDER or a DISTANCE relative to a group of objects, and the solver finds the layout from it. For a port at the far right: `{#M6 #M7 #CL} %pout @DISTANCE("left-to-right", min=2)`, or `{#M7 %pout} @ORDER("left-to-right")` when M7 is the rightmost already.
- **Members are the lowest objects**: instances, bundles, terminals and pins, points, TEXT (by its own position, 13.3), the centre of a RECTANGLE, CIRCLE or ELLIPSE written with a centre, and usages drawn with a symbol, each by its own point. A usage without a symbol is not a member itself but stands for its members: the objects that fill its roles, a member that is a usage without a symbol followed inward down to the lowest objects, as for GROUP (11.5), and the nets among them left out. Its own point is written `%u::position` (6.5). Wires, nets, constraints and definitions cannot be members: given one, the constraint fails. A position on a wire must first be written as a point (13.1). A set is not a member: it has no position of its own (below).
- **Which point counts**: an instance counts by its origin, the origin of its symbol, which is also what PLACE places; a terminal or a pin by the position of the pin, which is the position of the device plus the rigid transform of the pin position in the symbol and cannot be moved on its own; a point by itself; a usage with a symbol, or `::position` of any usage, by its own point (below); a TEXT by its own position; a RECTANGLE, CIRCLE or ELLIPSE written with a centre by its centre. The centre of the box of a symbol is not used: the boxes of three-terminal and four-terminal MOS symbols differ, and a row of them would not line up. To align by pins, write terminals. Visual symmetry requires the origin to lie on the axis of symmetry of the symbol, which is up to the symbol library.
- **The own point of a usage**: every usage has a point of its own, also a usage without a symbol. The point starts out free, like `(_, _)`, and is tied by facts: PLACE, constraints, wires to the pins of its symbol, and the facts that the body of its definition writes on it; the solver places what is left free. A usage with a symbol is drawn with the origin of its symbol at this point (12.1), and a constraint on it acts on this point. A constraint on a usage without a symbol acts on its members (above); to constrain its point, write `%u::position`: `{%dp::position #X1} @ALIGN("y")` puts X1 at the height of the centre of the pair, where `{%dp #X1} @ALIGN("y")` would put X1 and the three transistors of the pair at one height. The body of its definition ties the point to its members where it writes facts on the lone `?` (9.4), such as `{?members::position ?} @ALIGN("y")`. When the point of a usage is tied by no fact to anything drawn, a constraint on `%u::position` has no visible effect, and a hint says so.
- **Sets have no position**: a set is only a list of members, with no centre and no extent. In the member list of ALIGN, ORDER or DISTRIBUTE, an item that is a set, a `{…}` written out or a reference to a set (5.2), stands for each of its innermost members at that item: the statement is one constraint for each combination that takes one member from each such item. `{#M1 {#M2 #M3} #M4} @ORDER("top-to-bottom")` is `{#M1 #M2 #M4}` and `{#M1 #M3 #M4}`, so that M2 and M3 are each between M1 and M4 and are not ordered between themselves. An item that is a usage without a symbol stands for its members (above) in the same way. A set in a slot of DISTANCE stands for each of its members, one constraint for each pair (14.5). An item that is an empty set is left out. A combination that takes one object twice is left out: two structures that share a device, ordered as `{%stage1 %stage2} @ORDER("left-to-right")`, order every other pair of their members and do not order that device against itself.
  - The constraints so made have the priority, the name and the position of the statement written (8.5, 14.8); a name binds the set of them. A combination with too few members to say anything gives a hint.
  - An implementation may limit how many constraints one statement stands for; beyond its limit the statement fails, with an error that says it is a limit of the implementation (1.6).
  - A set that is the only object is split one level first (5.2.1): `%row_set @ORDER("left-to-right")` and `{%row_set} @ORDER("left-to-right")` both order the members of the set.
- **Directions are written out.** ALIGN takes a projection, `"x"` or `"y"` (14.2); ORDER, DISTRIBUTE and DISTANCE take a direction, one of four (14.3). No form leaves the direction open: a relation that may be drawn either way is written as one fact for one way, and a definition that should allow both drawings comes as two definitions, such as `$BRANCH` and `$BRANCH_HORIZONTAL` of the standard package. The only facts that offer a choice are the lists of orientations of ORIENT (11.2) and ORIENT-RELATION (14.9).
- Constraints do not change the netlist. In a conflict, an atomic constraint is kept whole or discarded whole (8.5).
- The four atomic constraints, ORIENT-RELATION and TABLE take the parameter `priority=` (14.8).

### 14.2 ALIGN

`{…} @ALIGN("y")`: the members are equal in the projection `axis`: `"y"` gives them the same y (a row), `"x"` the same x (a column). `axis` is required, can be given by position, and is one projection: a list of projections is a wrong parameter value, and the constraint fails. With fewer than two members, the constraint holds trivially and does not fail: how many members a selection or a set has is not known when the statement is written.

### 14.3 ORDER

```rnl
%rc_set = {#R2 #C2};
{#M1 #M1:2 #M2:2 #M2} @ORDER("left-to-right");  /* M1 on the left, the two gates face each other */
{#R1 {%rc_set} #M1} @ORDER("left-to-right")     /* R2 and C2 each between R1 and M1 */
```

- `{…} @ORDER(direction)`: the members are strictly monotonic in the projection of the direction. With fewer than two members, it holds trivially.
- **Direction**: the parameter `direction`, required, given by position first. Its values are:
  - `"left-to-right"`, `"right-to-left"`: increasing x, decreasing x;
  - `"bottom-to-top"`, `"top-to-bottom"`: increasing y, decreasing y (y goes up, 11.7).

  DISTRIBUTE and DISTANCE take the same values. There is no value that fixes only the axis: `"x"` and `"y"` are wrong parameter values, and the constraint fails.
- An item that is a set stands for each of its members (14.1): the second example orders R1, R2, M1 and R1, C2, M1; R2 and C2 are not ordered between themselves.
- ORDER is a fact on points: it compares the points of the members, not their boxes. Keeping the boxes of symbols apart along an ORDER is a criterion of the solver, ranked after every fact (8.5); when the facts force boxes to overlap, they overlap, and a hint may say so.
- ORDER is strict: neighbouring members differ by at least one coordinate unit (11.7). It is short for a DISTANCE in its direction with `min=1` between each pair of neighbouring members (14.5). For equal coordinates, use ALIGN.

### 14.4 DISTRIBUTE

```rnl
{#R1 #R2 #R3} @DISTRIBUTE("left-to-right")     /* R1, R2 and R3 from left to right, in equal steps */
```

- `{…} @DISTRIBUTE(direction)`: the members follow one another strictly in the direction, as for ORDER (14.3), and the differences between neighbouring members in its projection are equal. The steps are between the points of the members, not gaps between boxes; an item that is a set stands for each of its members (14.1).
- **Direction**: the parameter `direction`, required, given by position first, with the values of ORDER (14.3).
- With two members, DISTRIBUTE is the same as ORDER; with fewer than two, it holds trivially.

### 14.5 DISTANCE

```rnl
#M1:2 #M1 @DISTANCE("left-to-right", min=1);      /* the gate is on the left */
#R1 #R2 @DISTANCE("left-to-right", exact=8)       /* R2 exactly 8 units to the right of R1 */
```

```rnl
%stage1_set = {#M1 #M2 #M5};
%stage2_set = {#M3 #M4};
%stage1_set %stage2_set @DISTANCE("left-to-right", min=1)     /* all of stage 2 is to the right of all of stage 1 */
```

- `A B @DISTANCE(direction, min=…, max=…)`: A fills the slot `from` and B the slot `to`; each is an object or a set.
- **Direction**: the parameter `direction`, required, given by position first, with the values of ORDER (14.3). Only that axis is constrained; this is not the Euclidean distance.
  - An increasing direction (`"left-to-right"`, `"bottom-to-top"`) constrains B − A;
  - a decreasing direction (`"right-to-left"`, `"top-to-bottom"`) constrains A − B.
- **Sets**: a set in a slot stands for each of its members (14.1), and so does a usage without a symbol, and the DISTANCE holds from every member of A to every member of B, one constraint for each pair; a pair of one object with itself is left out. So `%stage1_set %stage2_set @DISTANCE("left-to-right", min=1)` puts all of stage 2 to the right of all of stage 1, and two sets that are not to interleave are written this way. When one side is an empty set, there is no pair, and nothing happens.
- At least one of `min`, `max` and `exact` must be written; `min` must not be greater than `max`. A DISTANCE also states the order: B is never before A in the direction. So the bounds are not negative, and a negative `min`, `max` or `exact` is a wrong parameter value, for which the constraint fails; a `min` that is not written is 0, so `max=10` holds for a distance from 0 to 10.
- `exact=` is an exact distance, short for equal `min` and `max`: B − A = exact for an increasing direction, A − B = exact for a decreasing one. It cannot be written together with `min` or `max`: that is an invalid parameter, and the statement fails. Tools write exact distances with `exact` (4.3).

### 14.6 Signal flow

Signal flow (Principles 2.4) is written as the usage of a general definition; there is no keyword for it. Whether the standard package provides such a definition, and under what name, is up to the standard package (16.3); the examples use `$SIGNAL_FLOW` of the standard series 0.4, which names a path, given as a set, and may name the sources before it and the loads after it. It is a semantic definition: it places nothing.

```rnl
%rc_set = {#R2 #C2} @GROUP;
{#R1 {%rc_set} #M1} @AS $SIGNAL_FLOW                                    /* the signal goes from R1 through the block of R2 and C2 to M1 */
{#:inp #:inn}(source) {#R1 {%rc_set} #M1}(path) #CL(load) @AS $SIGNAL_FLOW   /* from the two inputs of the level to the load CL */
```

- Signal flow is a soft notion: not every circuit has a strict direction. A solver may read it as a preference; to put the stages in order on the drawing, write ORDER or TABLE (14.3, 14.11).
- A stage of the path that is made of several objects is written as a `{…}` of its own, such as `{%rc_set}`. A GROUP (11.5) only makes the solver treat it as one block, and may be left out.
- The sources and the loads are each a group without an order, such as the two inputs of a differential signal. They may be devices, structures, ports of the level such as `#:inp` (6.4), other terminals, or nets. Without roles marked, the objects are the path, as in the first line.
- The flow of power, from a supply through the stages that convert or switch it to the parts that use it, is written in the same way with `$POWER_FLOW`.

### 14.7 Symmetry

Symmetry is written as the usage of a general definition. The axis of symmetry is only a free point, and no keyword is needed. The standard series 0.4 provides `$MIRRORED_LAYOUT`, with the roles `left`, `right` and `axis`: left and right at the same height, the axis between them in the middle, and right oriented as left flipped left-right; and `$MIRRORED_VERTICAL_LAYOUT`, with `top`, `bottom` and `axis`, the same turned upright. The own point of either usage is at the centre between the two sides (14.1), so that a constraint on the usage moves the pair. As in every definition of the standard package, these constraints are of priority `"low"` (9.5). The axis is one object: when several devices stand on the axis, they are made one usage first, which counts by its own point (14.1), or one of them is given.

```rnl
%ax = (_, _);
%sym12 = #M1(left) #M2(right) %ax(axis) @AS $MIRRORED_LAYOUT
```

- `$MIRRORED_LAYOUT` does not fix the actual orientation: whether the gates face inwards or outwards still needs an ORIENT on one side (14.9). For a symmetric pair with the same orientation on both sides, write another general definition, without its ORIENT-RELATION.

### 14.8 Priority: PRIORITY

```rnl id=dpl
$DP_LAYOUT = @DEFINE(roles=["in_p", "in_n"]) {
  {?in_p ?in_n} @ALIGN("y", priority="low")                    /* a default layout, the first to give way */
};
%dp2 = #M1(in_p) #M2(in_n) @AS(priority="high") $DP_LAYOUT;     /* this pair keeps the default layout strictly */
#M1 @PLACE(12, 20, priority="high")                             /* fixes the position of M1 */
```

```rnl use=dpl
# @SELECT-USAGE(recursive=true) $DP_LAYOUT => @PRIORITY("disabled")    /* a house-style package turns the default layout off, to apply its own */
```

- **Four values**: `"disabled"`, `"low"`, `"normal"`, `"high"`. A fact without a priority is `"normal"`, whether it is written in a record of the user, in a package or in the body of a definition.
- **Ranked, not weighted**: in a conflict, priorities are compared first, and the higher one is kept; with equal priorities, the one written later is kept (8.5). Constraints are not summed with weights.

  Note: with weights, ten constraints of low priority could outweigh one of high priority, and solvers differ in how they measure how far a constraint is violated.
- **Every fact that is kept is hard**: `"low"` means "discarded first in a conflict", not "as close as possible". The solver must satisfy every fact that is kept, whatever its priority; the implementation's own aims for a good-looking drawing are optimized only within the facts kept, after all descriptions of the language.
- **`"disabled"`**: the fact is removed before the ordering and is not given to the solver. This is not a failure: the object still exists, descriptions that depend on it are not affected, no warning is given, and a priority written later can turn it on again.
- **How it is written**:
  - PLACE, ORIENT, the four atomic constraints, ORIENT-RELATION, TABLE, ROUTE-NET, ROUTE-OBJECT, UNROUTE-NET, UNROUTE-OBJECT, AS and ATTACH take the parameter `priority=`, and the priority goes with the fact. `priority` is the only standard parameter of AS and ATTACH; a parameter of a definition cannot have this name.
  - The PRIORITY statement changes a priority afterwards. It takes only objects with a handle of their own: constraints, usages, and sets with GROUP (on and off only, 11.5). A set with GROUP is taken as the group itself; any other set is taken member by member (5.2.1). Given an instance or another object, it fails (wrong kind): `#M1 @PLACE(…) => @PRIORITY(…)` is an error, because PLACE passes on the handle of M1; write the priority as a parameter of PLACE.
  - The record head `@RNL(priority=…)` gives a default to the statements of the record that take this parameter and do not write it, and to the `dx` and `dy` of TEXT (13.3). To AS and ATTACH it gives only `"disabled"`, so that a disabled record turns off its usages with their bodies, like its other statements. Under a head with `"high"`, `"normal"` or `"low"`, a usage has a priority only when its own statement or a PRIORITY statement gives it one, and otherwise keeps the priorities of its body, as when a rule makes it, so that a usage that the solver writes out (10.5) means what the generated one meant. A priority that a usage writes still overrides its body (below).
- **Order of overriding**, from low to high: the default of the record head; the statement's own parameter; PRIORITY statements (among several, the later wins; for those in packages, by the position of the USE-PACKAGE); the priority of an enclosing usage; PRIORITY statements written from outside a usage, through `::`, directly on one of its local objects (among several, the later wins). When none of these is given, the priority is `"normal"`.
- **The priority of a usage goes down**: the priority of a usage (its own parameter, or a PRIORITY statement on it) applies to all facts that it expands to, including those of nested usages, and overrides their own priorities; with nesting, the outermost usage that has a priority decides. To adjust only a part inside, write directly on that local object from outside, through `::` (9.4): this is more specific than the priority of the usage and overrides it: the more specific one wins. Such a statement written in the body of an enclosing definition is still subject to the priority of the enclosing usage. Every statement of a record can be changed directly, so the record head gives only a default.
  - In full: the priority of a usage governs every PRIORITY statement written in the body of its definition, which then has no effect. A PRIORITY written from outside through `::` directly on a local usage governs in the same way: that usage counts as the outermost one with a priority, and the statement decides for everything that the usage expands to. Among the statements that nothing governs, the one written on the innermost object wins; on the same object, one written from outside the body that makes the object wins over one written in that body; then the later one wins.
  - For example, with `%o1` disabled, `%o1::in @PRIORITY("high")` at the top and `%in::row @PRIORITY("low")` in the body of the definition of `%o1`, the facts of `row` are `"high"`: the statement in the body is governed by the priority of `%o1`, which the statement at the top overrides for `in`. If `%o1` had no priority, they would be `"low"`, written on the more inner object. To have `"low"` in both cases, write `%o1::in::row @PRIORITY("low")` from outside.
- **The output of the solver**: the solver writes its results as PLACE, ORIENT and so on, with `"high"`, in the last record of the level's own text, such as `@RNL(type="generated", priority="high") {…}`. For a subcircuit, this is inside its `.subckt … .ends`, even when that is in an included file; for the top level, it is in the file level of the main file (chapter 17). When the solver solves, the level is interpreted without the records with `type="generated"` (2.8), so old results do not constrain the next solve; after solving, it removes them and writes the new results at the end.
  - Between solves, a new statement of normal priority that conflicts with the results of the solver is discarded and takes effect only after the next solve. A viewer may suggest solving again, by the hash in the record head; such discards need not be reported one by one.
  - A drag in an editor is written as a PLACE with `"high"`, also in the level's own text, after the record of the solver. This fixes the position: every later solve takes it as a hard constraint. To release it, delete that statement.
- **What the solver writes.** The record of the solver must hold every position, orientation and route that the solver decided and that the text leaves free: by name or key for the objects that have one, by number for the others (5.9), with WIRE for the route of a wire (13.1), and as explicit statements for the objects that rules generate (10.5). A decision that is not written there is not a result: the object stays free, as if the level had not been solved. So a viewer that does not solve draws the level exactly as the solver drew it. The solver does not decide what it cannot refer to, such as the position of a text without a key that a generation rule makes directly; the viewer places such a text (13.3). The own point of a usage that a rule makes is not written either when that usage cannot be written out, as when its definition is private to a package (16.2), has no symbol and no text, and no other object or wire is placed by its point: such a point only helps the solver, nothing drawn stands on it, and leaving it free changes no drawing; it does not count as a decision left unwritten.
- **The results of a solver are RNL.** When a solver passes its results to other tools in another form, such as the messages between a solver and a viewer, that form must hold only what a record of the solver can hold, so that every result can be saved as such a record.
- **The objectives of a solver** choose only among the layouts that satisfy every fact that is kept; a fact that is kept is hard, whatever its priority. The objectives are not part of the language: the kinds of objectives and their order differ between solvers, and this standard cannot define a common vocabulary for them. One default is set: an `"auto"` wire with nothing in its way and not merged with other wires is solved to its default route (13.1), which counts bends first and length second. Solver options are part of the input that the hash covers (chapter 17); a file keeps them with SOLVER-CONFIG, so that a result can be reproduced (14.10).

### 14.9 Orientation relation: ORIENT-RELATION

```rnl
#M1 #M2 @ORIENT-RELATION("MY");                  /* M2 is drawn as M1 flipped left-right */
#M5 {#M7 #M8} @ORIENT-RELATION("R0");            /* two facts: M7 and M8 each have the orientation of M5 */
#M3 #M4 @ORIENT-RELATION(["R0", "MY"])           /* M4 as M3, or as M3 flipped left-right */
#M1::g #M2::g @ORIENT-RELATION("R180")           /* the gates of M1 and M2 face opposite ways */
```

- `A B @ORIENT-RELATION(relation)`: two slots, `from` and `to`, each one object. The relation is one of the eight orientation codes of ORIENT (11.2), or a list of them, given by position. The orientation of B is the orientation of A followed by the transform of the relation. The transform is taken in the coordinates of the drawing, so `"MY"` always flips left-right about a vertical line, whether or not A itself is rotated.
- **A list of relations**, such as `["R0", "MY"]`, makes one fact, which holds when one of the listed relations holds between A and B. It is kept or discarded as a whole (8.5), and the solver chooses which relation it draws. The list has at least one code; a list of one code is the same as that code.
- **One object to one object.** A set in one slot is done member by member (5.2.1), one fact for each member: `#M5 {#M7 #M8} @ORIENT-RELATION("R0")` gives M7 and M8 each the orientation of M5, and so all three the same. A set of more than one member in both slots is an error: pairing the members in order and relating every pair would both make sense (5.2.1).
- There is no form with a single set whose members all have the same orientation: the keyword names a relation between two things, and with one set it is not clear what the relation applies to. A single object does not match the signature, which is an error. A single set is first split one level, as 5.2.1 says, so `{#M1 #M2} @ORIENT-RELATION("MY")` is `#M1 #M2 @ORIENT-RELATION("MY")`; when the split does not give two objects, it is an error.
- Members are objects that have an orientation, as for ORIENT (11.2): instances, bundles and usages with a symbol; or both are pins (below), a terminal standing for its pin (5.2.4). A point, net, wire or usage without a symbol is an error, and so is a pin related to an object: the constraint fails. When one slot gets an empty set, there is nothing to relate, and nothing happens (5.2.1).
- **Pins.** With a pin in each slot, the relation holds between the directions of the two pins in the drawing (11.2): the direction of B is the direction of A turned by the relation, such as `"R180"` for pins that face opposite ways, and a mirrored code turns a direction as it turns a point. The relation is written with the codes only: the words of 11.2 name directions, not turns, and written here they are wrong parameter values, so the constraint fails. A pin without a written `direction=` makes that fact fail (9.2.4).
- It is a constraint: it makes a constraint object, can be named, takes `priority=` and can be changed with PRIORITY (14.8); in conflicts it is kept or discarded as one unit (8.5). Done member by member, it makes one constraint for each member, and a name binds the set of them (5.2.1). A member with an ORIENT of one code has its orientation fixed already; for the other members, the solver chooses among the orientations that meet the relation and their ORIENT lists (11.2).
- It does not fix the actual orientation: when two MOS transistors are mirror images, gates facing inwards and gates facing outwards both meet `"MY"`. To decide, write one ORIENT as well, or an ORDER on terminals (14.3); to narrow it without deciding, write ORIENT with a list (11.2).

### 14.10 Solver settings: SOLVER-CONFIG

```rnl header
@SOLVER-CONFIG(spacing_x=2, spacing_y=1, layout="lines", lines_weights=[1, 4])    /* the default of the file */
```

```rnl
@SOLVER-CONFIG(spacing_x=1)                                                       /* this level: overrides the key spacing_x */
```

- SOLVER-CONFIG gives settings to the solver, so that the file can be solved again with the same settings. It makes no facts: the solver uses the settings only to choose among the layouts that satisfy every fact kept (14.8).
- **Form**: named parameters in RNL literals: numbers, strings, `true` and `false`, and arrays of these (3.7). There are no nested settings. Apart from the keys that this standard names (below), the keys are not standard words: each solver defines its own. The core reads the parameters as RNL literals and passes them to the solver as written, in the order below, and merges none of them away; of the values, it checks only those of the keys that this standard names. The solver judges the other keys and values: a key it does not know gives a warning and is ignored. A solver may also take a key only under some of its own settings, such as an objective that one of its layouts uses and another does not; under a setting that does not use the key, the key is ignored in the same way, with a warning.
- **Keys named by this standard**: `spacing_x` and `spacing_y`, the spacing of the level in grid units, added to the default spacing of the solver, as for groups (11.5); and `junction`, the definition with which the solver draws the dots at branch points, a `$` name written as a string, such as `"$JUNCTION"`; in the SOLVER-CONFIG of a level record also a `%` name, looked up in that level (6.6), so that a variant defined for one subcircuit stays in it; or `""` for no dots (13.2). Every solver takes `junction`. They are the only keys this version names, not the only keys a solver may take: which other keys a solver has, such as `layout` and `lines_weights` in the example, and what they mean, is up to that solver, which documents them. A solver that has a spacing of the level takes it by these two names.
- **Where**: in the header record without `language`, for the levels of that file and of the files it includes, as for DEVICE (2.8, 6.12); in the header record of a package file, for the levels that use the package (16.2); and in the records of a level, for that level. It has no objects. Written in the content of a package, in an inline package, in a record with `language` or in a record of the solver, it has no effect, and a warning is given; so a package used only through header records with `language` gives no settings.
- **Order**: settings override key by key, the later over the earlier: the defaults of the solver, the header records of the packages used by the level, in the order of their positions (as for DEVICE, 6.12), the header records of the outer files, then those of the included files, the records of the level, then the settings given to the tool, such as on its command line or in a page of a viewer. A tool takes those in the same text as the brackets, so that they can be copied either way.
- **A value that is not taken**, such as a string where a number is taken, is an error for that key only (16.4): the key counts as not written in that place, so the value from before stays in effect, and the other keys of the same statement apply as usual. A value is judged in each level that it applies to: a `junction` in a header record that names no definition in one level is not taken in that level alone, and the other levels still use it. The core judges the values of the keys that this standard names, and leaves out those it does not take; the solver judges the values of its own keys. So the settings a solver receives are a list in the order above, in which a key may come more than once, and for each key the solver takes the last value that it accepts.
- **Budgets of time or work** (8.5) are given only by the tool, such as on its command line or in a page of a viewer, never by the file: a budget depends on the machine that solves. The keys of a solver that set a budget, such as `limit`, `seconds` and `work` of the built-in solver, are its tool-only keys: written in SOLVER-CONFIG, in any record, such a key is ignored, with a warning, as an unknown key is, and the budget given by the tool, or the solver's default, stays in effect.
- The settings in effect for a level, those of packages included, are part of the input that the hash of the solver covers (chapter 17).

### 14.11 Tables: TABLE

```rnl
@TABLE layout: {
  {#M1 #M2 #M3}
  {#M4 {#M5 #M6} #M7}
}
```

- TABLE lays out objects as a table written with nested `{…}`: the example has two rows from top to bottom; in the first, M1, M2 and M3 from left to right; in the second, M4, the cell of M5 and M6, and M7 from left to right; in that cell, M5 above M6. A table may be written over several lines. The standard form writes the keyword first, read as "@TABLE layout: {…}" (4.3); the word `layout:` there is free text (3.3), and the slot of TABLE that takes the table is `rows` (Annex A).
- **Reading**: the outer list runs from top to bottom, one item for each row; each list nested in it runs at right angles to the list that holds it, from left to right in a list that runs from top to bottom, and from top to bottom in one that runs from left to right. `transpose=true` makes the outer list run from left to right. The set given to TABLE is its outer list as written and is not split first (5.2.1 item 2), so `@TABLE layout: {{#M1 #M2 #M3}}` is one row, and `@TABLE layout: %port_set` puts each item of the set in a row of its own.
- **Items**: an item that is an object is one cell; an item that is a set, a `{…}` written in the table or a reference to a set, is a list one level further in. This is the rule of TABLE: a set in it is not an item that stands for each of its members (14.1). A usage drawn with a symbol is one cell, by its own point; a usage without a symbol is one cell that holds its members (14.1), every one of them before the next item; `%u::position` makes its own point a cell.
- **The fact**: a TABLE is one geometric fact, kept or discarded as a whole in conflicts (8.5). It holds when, in every list of the table, each point of an item lies strictly before each point of the next item, in the direction of that list: at least one coordinate unit before, as for ORDER (14.3). A checker may expand it into such orders; the solver is given the table as one item, with the direction of its outer list and the lists nested in it.
- **A group**: the objects of the whole table are one group, as with `@GROUP` on the same set (11.5); its rows and columns are not groups of their own.
- TABLE does not align: rows need not have one height, nor columns one x. To align them, write ALIGN. A cell does not span several rows or columns.
- An empty list in a table is left out, and its neighbours become neighbours (14.1). A table of one cell holds trivially and makes no group. An object in two cells of one table would have to come before itself, so the TABLE fails, as it does when a member cannot be located, such as a net or a terminal without a pin (14.1).
- TABLE takes `priority=` (14.8) and makes a constraint object, which can be named.
