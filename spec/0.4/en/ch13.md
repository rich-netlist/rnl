## 13 Wires and text

### 13.1 Wires: WIRE

```rnl id=w13
&x @WIRE(mode="straight") {#M1:1 (10, 37) #M3:1} => @SET-STYLE $signal => %w13;
&out @WIRE(mode="manhattan") {#M2:1 #M4:1};
&tail @WIRE {#M1:3 #M5:1}                    /* no mode: "auto", routed freely */
```

- WIRE takes a net (or a set of nets, which makes a bus; see below) and an ordered list of points, at least two. A point is a point object or a pin, including a pin of a virtual part; a terminal stands for its pin (5.2.4). Points may repeat; when the first and the last point are the same, the wire is a loop.
- WIRE does not take the result of a selection, or a set that depends on one (10.4): given one, it fails. A wire is written back by the solver as that wire, with its bends (14.8), and a wire made from a selection would have nothing to be written back as; to draw the pins of a selection as wires, use ROUTE-OBJECT (13.7).
- **Routing mode**: the wire passes through every point of the list; how it runs between two neighbouring points is set by the parameter `mode=`. For neighbouring points A(x1, y1) and B(x2, y2), with dx = x2 − x1 and dy = y2 − y1:

  | `mode` | From A to B |
  |---|---|
  | `"auto"` (default) | Decided by the solver, which may optimize it for the situation and writes the route back (see below, 14.8); before the wire is solved, it is drawn by the default route below, which is also what the solver gives it when nothing is in its way |
  | `"straight"` | One straight segment |
  | `"horizontal-vertical"` | A → (x2, y1) → B |
  | `"vertical-horizontal"` | A → (x1, y2) → B |
  | `"manhattan"` | If \|dx\| ≥ \|dy\|: half of dx across, all of dy up or down, then the other half across: A → (x1 + dx/2, y1) → (x1 + dx/2, y2) → B; otherwise the same with x and y exchanged |
  | `"manhattan45"` | If \|dx\| ≥ \|dy\|: (\|dx\| − \|dy\|)/2 across, then a 45° diagonal (\|dy\| along each axis), then the rest across; otherwise the same with x and y exchanged |

  - Every mode except `"auto"` is a strict rule, and the viewer draws it as defined. When dx or dy is 0, every mode gives one straight segment. When |dx| = |dy|, `"manhattan"` takes the case |dx| ≥ |dy|, and `"manhattan45"` is one diagonal segment. The bends that a mode computes keep their exact values and are not rounded; they may fall on half units (11.7).
  - **The default route of `"auto"`**: each segment between two neighbouring points runs horizontally and vertically. An end that is a pin with a direction (9.2.4), taken after the orientation of its object, is directed: the segment leaves it along that direction or at a right angle to it, and arrives at it against that direction or at a right angle to it, never the other way; leaving or arriving at a right angle counts as a bend. A pin without a direction and a point are undirected and constrain nothing. The segment passes through neither of its ends and does not overlap itself. Among the routes that satisfy this, the default is the one with the fewest bends; then the shortest, where a leg that goes out only to come back is 1 long; then the one that turns at fewer of its directed ends, keeping the direction of the first end before that of the last; then the one whose middle leg lies halfway between the ends, as for `"manhattan"`; between two undirected ends, the one that goes horizontally first, an L with one bend; and a detour goes to the left of the first directed end (its direction turned counterclockwise by 90 degrees). A wire with points between its ends is chosen as a whole: its middle points are undirected, a turn at a middle point counts as a bend, and the wire may not turn back at a middle point, so it goes straight through one where it can. Its bends, too, keep their exact values and may fall on half units (11.7), as when its middle leg lies halfway between two ends one unit apart.
  - The bends added by a mode are not objects: they cannot be referenced, and no junction can be put on them. To branch off at a bend or to draw a dot there, write the bend as a point. A free point `(_, _)` first gets its coordinates from the solver and is then connected according to the mode.
  - A `mode` value that is not in the table is an invalid parameter value: the wire fails.
- **Style parameters**: WIRE also takes the line style properties as parameters: `linecolor`, `linestyle`, `linewidth`, `cap`, `join` (9.3). They are the direct style of the wire (8.3), such as `@WIRE(linestyle="bold")` for a supply rail or `@WIRE(linestyle="dashed")` for a substrate connection.
- **Rewriting a wire.** Given a wire in place of a net, WIRE makes no new wire: it rewrites that wire. The statement is read as a new WIRE would be, and its list of points and its mode replace those of the wire; the net of the wire stays. Style parameters are direct style of the wire (8.3).
  - The rewrite has a key, the wire: a later one replaces the list and the mode of an earlier one (8.4); of two segments of one pipe, the later segment is the later one (5.5). The style parameters of every rewrite stay direct style of the wire and are resolved by 8.3, property by property. A rewrite is not a geometric fact (8.5).
  - A rewrite that fails, such as one that lists a terminal without a pin (9.2.4), is an error and has no effect, its style parameters included; the wire keeps its earlier list and mode: those of an earlier rewrite, or those it was made with (5.8). Each rewrite that fails is reported, also when a later one replaces it.
  - Like a new wire, a rewrite counts from step 3 of 8.2: `wired=` (10.2) reads the points of the last rewrite, also when that rewrite fails later, after step 7 (7.1).
  - The net slot takes a net, a set of nets or one wire; a set that contains a wire is of the wrong kind.
  - The solver writes the route that it chose for each `"auto"` wire this way (14.8). It should keep, in order, the points that the wire has in the authored records, after their last rewrite of it, add only bends written as coordinates between them, and write `mode="straight"`: the connections of a wire are written by its author, and the solver decides only its geometry.
  - **Splitting a wire.** On a net that a tree of ROUTE-NET or ROUTE-OBJECT joins (13.7), the solver may branch from the middle of a wire that the author wrote. It then rewrites that wire in its record, by name or by number (5.9), keeping the author's points in their order and inserting the branch point among them as a point of its own, and connects the branch there. Other wires it changes only as the item above says. This is a rewrite, not a deletion: the authored statement stays.
  - An editor in which the user reshapes a wire writes the same in its own record; the solver then keeps the wire as rewritten.
  - To fix the route in the statement itself, write the bends as points and use a mode other than `"auto"`; an exporter that keeps the exact shape writes `mode="straight"` and lists every bend.
- **Several terminals on one net** (Principles 2.6) are connected by several wires that share points. Wires are connected only when they share the same point object (a named point) or the same terminal; equal coordinates do not connect them (Principles 2.2). There is no statement that writes a tree, and no graph object.
- **T junction**: the through wire lists the junction point as one of its intermediate points and need not be split in two; the branch starts at that point (see the example in 13.2). There is no "point on a segment of a wire".
- **Pins that touch**: when two pins are drawn at the same point with no wire between them, a wire is still written to connect the two terminals, such as for a ground symbol drawn right on the pin of a capacitor: `&0 @WIRE(mode="straight") {#C1:2 %g3::pin}`. Its two ends coincide and its length is 0, so nothing is drawn, but it states the connection: both pins are wired (10.2), and the standard package does not add another ground. Equal coordinates alone do not connect (Principles 2.2). An exporter that converts a schematic in which pins connect by position writes such wires; where a wire runs straight through a pin or a junction, it lists that point as an intermediate point (T junction); where a junction has only one wire and lies right on a pin, it connects the wire to that terminal directly.
- **The net is only stated**, and it must be written. The nets that the endpoints are actually on need not agree with it, and this is not checked (Principles 2.2); the net is not derived from the endpoints either, and the endpoints may be on different nets.
  - When a terminal or pin in the list is not on the net of the wire (for a bus wire: not in its set of nets), this gives a warning: most likely the terminal is mistyped, such as `#R1:1` for `#R1:2`. The net of a terminal or pin is known (a pin of a virtual part is on the net that fills the role it binds, 12.1); a point, and a pin that binds nothing, have no net and are not checked. The wire is valid and is drawn (Principles 2.2, 7.6).
- **A wire on no net**: the net slot also takes the empty node, as in `& @WIRE {%sub1::y %int1::a}` (6.3). The wire is drawn and solved as any other, and the pins it ends on are wired (10.2), but it belongs to no net: two such wires that share a point are drawn joined and are still not one net, no tree of ROUTE-NET or ROUTE-OBJECT takes them in, and `${net}` of the wire is the empty text. A terminal or pin in its list that is on a net gives the warning above, and so does a pin on the empty node in the list of a wire of a net: the drawing would show a connection that the circuit does not have. The empty node cannot be an item of the set of a bus.
- **Buses**: the net slot takes a net, or a set of nets as a whole. Given a set, the wire is a bus wire, also when the set has one member. A netlist has no buses (6.11); a bus is only a way of drawing several nets as one line.

  ```spice
  * A 4-bit bus between two blocks, with two taps
  * @RNL(type="header") { @VERSION("0.4"); @USE-PACKAGE(name="rnl-std-all", version="0.4")}
  .subckt blk D<3> D<2> D<1> D<0>
  RL D<3> D<0> 1k
  * @RNL {
  *   $BLK_SYM = @DEFINE-SYMBOL {
  *     (-3, 0) @PIN(terminal=["D<3>", "D<2>", "D<1>", "D<0>"], label="D<3:0>", direction="left");
  *     @RECTANGLE {(-3, -2) (3, 2)}
  *   };
  *   # @SET-SYMBOL $BLK_SYM                                             /* one bus pin for four ports (9.2.4) */
  * }
  .ends blk
  XA D<3> D<2> D<1> D<0> blk
  XB D<3> D<2> D<1> D<0> blk
  RT3 D<3> 0 1k
  RT2 D<2> 0 1k
  * @RNL {
  *   %t3 = (20, 10); %t2 = (24, 10);
  *   %bus = &D<3:0> @WIRE(linestyle="bold") {#XA:D<3> %t3 %t2 #XB:D<3>};   /* a bus between the bus pins of XA and XB */
  *   %bus @TEXT("D<3:0>");                                                /* the name of the bus, written out */
  *   &D<3> @WIRE {%t3 #RT3:1};                                            /* a tap: a single wire that shares %t3 with the bus */
  *   &D<2> @WIRE(mode="straight") {#RT2:1 (23, 9) %t2}                    /* its last stretch enters the bus at 45° */
  * }
  .end
  ```

  - The order of the set is the order of the bits, and duplicates are kept; sets written directly inside the set are taken down to their innermost members, in order. A member that is not a net makes the wire fail (wrong kind). An empty set fails too: a wire carries at least one net (5.2.1).
  - The net slot is strict (7.3): when one bit cannot be found, the whole wire fails, as when a single net cannot be found.
  - Points, `mode=`, style parameters, junctions and T junctions are as for other wires. There is no BUS keyword: an implementation draws a bus as it draws a wire, and drawing it bold is a matter of style (1.5 item 7). An importer may write `linestyle="bold"` directly; the standard package makes bus wires bold by default with the condition `bus=true` of SELECT-WIRE (10.1, 16.3).
  - **Taps** need no mechanism: a single wire that shares a point with a bus is a tap, and the bus lists the tap point as an intermediate point (T junction). A short 45° stretch into the bus is only the way the last stretch of the single wire runs. A tap symbol is a definition applied at the tap point and belongs to the standard package (16.3). The name of the bit is a label `${net}` on the single wire (13.3). Whether the net of the single wire is in the bus is not checked (Principles 2.2); an implementation may give a hint.
  - A tap can also start at an end of a bus: a bus that ends at a point may go on as several buses or single wires that start from that point, such as a trunk `BOT<10:0>` that ends where `BOT<10:3>` and `BOT<2:0>` go off, with a junction there (13.2). The trunk lists that point as an end, and each part takes its own bits. Wires that start from one point where a wire that carries all their nets also passes or ends, here the trunk, are taps of that wire and are not joined to each other: an implementation that warns when a wire ends on a wire of another net, as if the two were joined, does not warn about them.
  - The attribute `net` of a bus wire is the names of its nets in order, joined with `,` and no spaces, such as `D<3>,D<2>,D<1>,D<0>`; to show `D<3:0>`, write that text. Implementations do not shorten the list: shortening depends on how the host writes names and has no unique result.
  - So `{&a &b} @WIRE {…}` is one bus wire, not two wires drawn on top of each other.
  - Several host instances drawn as one (an iterated instance) are written as a bundle (11.8).
- A terminal that a wire connects to must have a pin in its symbol; otherwise the wire fails (9.2.4).
- Lines that touch, cross or overlap are only geometry and make no connection (Principles 2.2). Wires that cross without sharing a point get no dot; small arcs at crossings and the like are a matter of style and of the implementation.
- Each WIRE given a net is a new object without a key: written twice, it is two wires (5.8). To refer to a wire from another statement, name it; the record of the solver refers to a wire without a name by its number (5.9). An endpoint that is a terminal moves with its device; a point written with coordinates does not move; a named point can be constrained.
- **Connections not drawn**: how a connection is shown that has no wire in the RNL (routed by the solver and written back as WIRE, a flight line, a label) is decided by the house style of the solver and the viewer and by the house-style packages in use; the language does not specify it. A house-style package can make labels or wires with rules, and these are RNL. To have a net drawn as wires with the routing left to the solver, write ROUTE-NET or ROUTE-OBJECT (13.7).

### 13.2 Junctions

```rnl
%j1 = (10, 30);
&x @WIRE {#M1:1 %j1 #M3:1};          /* the through wire passes %j1 */
&x @WIRE {%j1 (30, 30) #M4:2};       /* the branch starts at %j1 */
$JUNCTION @ATTACH %j1                /* draws the dot */
```

- A branch is several wires that share one point (13.1).
- Junction dots are written explicitly: a dot is drawn only where a definition that draws one, such as `$JUNCTION` of the standard package, is applied; a renderer does not add dots by itself.
- A dot is a usage, not a keyword. `$JUNCTION` is a definition of the standard package with one role, which takes a point or a terminal; its definition ties the point of the usage to that point or to the pin of the terminal (12.1 item 5), as for `$NO_CONNECT` (12.6) and the current arrow (15.2). It can be applied to a terminal when two wires meet at its pin. The standard package gives these usages a filled dot as their symbol: the size of the dot comes from the symbol, and its colour from the `linecolor` of the style of the usage; there is no separate setting for the size of dots.
  - Applying `$JUNCTION` twice to the same point gives two dots on top of each other, which look like one.
  - All junctions are selected with `# @SELECT-USAGE $JUNCTION`.
  - The drawing order follows the position of the rule that assigns the symbol (11.6). The rules of the standard package come first, so the dots are drawn before the wires; a dot has the colour of the wires and is wider than they are, so this makes no visible difference, and no LAYER is needed.
- Dots are not derived from the number of wires that meet at a shared point: real schematics have branch points with only two wires that are drawn with a dot, and a derived dot would need yet another way to remove it. An exporter writes the dots that the original shows. An implementation may give a hint where three or more wires meet at a shared point without a dot; this standard does not require it.
- **Dots added by the solver**: the output of the solver is RNL too and may contain dots. The definition it uses is given by the key `junction` of SOLVER-CONFIG (14.10), a `$` name written as a string, such as `junction="$JUNCTION"`, or, in the SOLVER-CONFIG of a level record, a `%` name of that level, such as `junction="%BIG_DOT"` for a larger dot defined for that subcircuit alone; the standard package sets `$JUNCTION` for the levels that use it (16.3). With the key, the solver applies that definition to each branch point of the trees of ROUTE-NET and ROUTE-OBJECT (13.7), and where wires written by the user meet three or more at a shared point without a usage of that definition, it may add one in its output as well. Without the key, it writes no dots, and `junction=""` turns them off where a package has set it. The name is looked up as a name written in the record of the solver in that level (6.8); any other value that names no definition there, or one whose first role takes no point, is a value that is not taken in that level (14.10): an error, and the key counts as not written there, so the value from before applies, or, if there is none, the solver writes no dots. A shared point is always a named point or a terminal (equal coordinates do not count), so the dot can be written. Other tools that add dots, such as an editor, take the definition from the same key.
- A dot is only drawing: it makes no connection and is not checked (Principles 2.2). A point with `$JUNCTION` and no wire still gets its dot.

### 13.3 Text: TEXT

```rnl use=w13
(50, 18) @TEXT("5T OTA testbench");                         /* an annotation: attached to a point */
(1, 1) @TEXT("see here") => @PLACE(2, 2);                   /* attached to (1, 1); the text itself is at (2, 2) */
#M1 @TEXT("W/L=${w}/${l}", key="wl") => @PLACE(12, 18);     /* a label: attached to M1, with the key wl, so it is #M1::wl */
#R1 @TEXT("R_{D}", key="name");                             /* a key the symbol has: new content, the position still from the symbol */
#M1::name @PLACE(13, 18);                                   /* the name of M1, dragged in an editor */
%w13 @TEXT("${net}");                                       /* the net name on a wire; no key; placed by the implementation */
&out @TEXT("${name}");                                      /* shows the name of the net out; placed by the implementation */
# @SELECT-INSTANCE => @SELECT-PIN(wired=false) => @TEXT("${net}", key="netname")    /* net names on pins without wires */
```

- **Empty text**: a TEXT whose content is the empty string draws nothing. With a key, it replaces the label of the symbol with that key and so hides it. The standard package `rnl-std-no-labels` hides default labels with the style `textvisible=false` instead (9.3, 16.3), which keeps their content, so that a TEXT with the same key and `textvisible=true` shows one again.
- **Attachment**: TEXT takes a point, or an object that is drawn, and the text is attached to it. What it is attached to decides three things: whose attributes `${…}` reads (13.6); where the text goes when its position is not given; and, when it has a key, which object the text is a sub-object of.
  - A point (`(x, y)`, a named point, a `::position`): the text is an annotation (13.4). It can refer only to the attributes `x` and `y` of the point.
  - An object: the text is a label. The object can be an instance, a drawn usage, a bundle, a pin, a terminal (at its pin, 5.2.4), a wire or a graphic. It can also be a net: a net itself is not drawn, and the implementation puts a text attached to a net next to one of its wires or terminals. This is how a file says which nets show their names.
  - A usage without a symbol, or a terminal without a pin, has no meaning for drawing: given one, the TEXT fails (12.1, 9.2.4).
  - Given a set, TEXT attaches a text to each member (5.2.1), as in the last line of the example.
  - In a symbol body, a text attached to a point is fixed text of the symbol; a text attached to a lone `?` is attached to the object drawn with the symbol (see below).
- **Content**: `text` is required, also when a key is given; without it the TEXT fails. It can be given by position, first, as in `@TEXT("${name}", key="name")`. There is no default content: to show the name, write `"${name}"`. Markup and attribute references are in 13.6.
- **Position**: a text has a position of its own, which is not the point or object it is attached to.
  - The position of a text starts out free, like `(_, _)`. When no PLACE, `dx`, `dy` or constraint fixes it, the solver decides and writes the position back (14.8); without a solution, the viewer decides: a text attached to a point usually goes at that point, and a text attached to an object next to the object; either may be moved away to avoid overlaps.
  - PLACE sets the position of the text itself. So `(1, 1) @TEXT(…) => @PLACE(2, 2)` is not a contradiction: the first part attaches the text to (1, 1), the second puts the text at (2, 2). To put an annotation exactly somewhere, write PLACE.
  - `dx`, `dy`: offsets, taken only by TEXT. When what the text is attached to has a coordinate point (a point, the origin of an instance, the point where a usage is drawn, the pin of a terminal), the text goes at that point plus (dx, dy). Wires and nets have no coordinate point: an offset on a text attached to one makes the TEXT fail.
    - The text still has one position, the one with the offset; constraints and the solver use it, and there is no position "before the offset". When the attached object is moved or placed by the solver, the text moves with it; when other constraints move the text, the attached object moves with it. An offset is the same as one exact DISTANCE for x and one for y, written on the text: a positive offset in the increasing direction (`"left-to-right"`, `"bottom-to-top"`), a negative one in the decreasing direction, with its size as `exact` (14.5).
    - Offsets go along the axes of the scene and do not turn with the orientation of the attached object, since texts in the scene do not turn with objects. In a symbol body they can be written too; there they are in local coordinates and transform with the instance, like a local position written with `=> @PLACE(…)`.
    - **Offsets and PLACE replace each other**: on each axis, the position of a text is either absolute (the x or y of PLACE) or an offset (`dx` or `dy`), never both. On the same axis of the same text, the kind written later (by the position of 8.5) takes effect, and the kind written earlier has no effect at all: after an offset, the absolute position on that axis is free and follows from the offset; after a PLACE, the offset on that axis no longer applies. This is replacement, not a conflict: priority plays no part, and no hint is given. For example, after `dx` and `dy`, a later `#M1::name @PLACE(x=13)` makes x the absolute 13, `dx` no longer applies, and y still follows `dy`; an editor writes this when a text placed by offset is dragged. A position given in the symbol comes first (see below), and a PLACE or an offset in the scene replaces it.
    - Whichever takes effect is an ordinary geometric fact: the x and the y of an offset are one unit each, their position is that of the TEXT statement that writes them, and their priority is the default of the record head (14.8); they are resolved with other facts by 8.5. Several PLACEs on the same axis are resolved among themselves by 8.5. For a text with a key, `dx` and `dy` are overridden separately (8.4).
  - A text can be a member of constraints by its own position (14.1). The `::position` of a text is its own position: `%n1::position @PLACE(…)` is the same as `%n1 @PLACE(…)`.
  - Texts in the scene do not turn with the orientation of what they are attached to; to turn one, write `rotate=`. When the scene supplies both axes of a text's position, by PLACE or offsets, neither axis uses the symbol's local position: the text does not inherit the attached object's orientation, and `halign` and `valign` are interpreted in the scene's directions.
  - The solver writes back the position it chose (14.8): by the key (below) or the name of the text, or otherwise by its number (5.9). A text without a key that a generation rule makes directly (13.3, 10.5) cannot be referred to: the solver leaves its position to the viewer. A text in the body of a usage that a rule makes is referred to by number once the solver writes the usage as an explicit statement (10.5, 5.9).
- **Key `key=`**:
  - Without a key, the text is a text of the attached object that has no key; written again, it is another text, as with wires (13.1). It can be named with `%x =` and referred to and changed in its level. Texts made by rules without a key have no handle, so the solver cannot write back their positions.
  - With a key, the text is the sub-object `object::key` of the attached object, such as `#M1::wl`. The same object with the same key is the same text, and later statements override it parameter by parameter: the size, the alignment, the rotation and so on are each overridden on their own, and parameters not written stay as they were (8.4). The content is written in every statement (`text` is required), and the later content always replaces the earlier; to change only the alignment or the rotation, write the content again, as in `#M1 @TEXT("${name}", key="name", halign="end")`. The position follows the rules of PLACE (8.5). Other records, editors and the solver find the text by this handle.
  - A text attached to a point takes no `key=`, since a point has no sub-objects: with a key, the TEXT fails.
  - The names after `::` are looked up in a fixed order, and keys come last (6.5): the role names and local names of a usage, then their `definition=` aliases, then the standard sub-object names and their aliases, then the names of the pins of the object's symbol, then the keys of texts. So a key must not be spelled like any name that comes before it: a standard sub-object name (`position`, `net`) or an alias of one declared for the file (such as `位置` of `alias-zh`); a role name or local name of the usage the text is attached to; or a `definition=` alias of one of these. Such a key is an error, like the name clashes of 6.5. For a text written in a symbol, the aliases are those of the file in which the symbol is written. `name` and `value` are not standard sub-object names, only common keys.
- **Texts in symbols**:
  - In a symbol body, a lone `?` is the object drawn with the symbol: M1 when M1 is drawn, `%g0` when the usage `%g0` is drawn. It can only be the object of TEXT; anywhere else it fails, such as `? @PLACE(…)` in a symbol, which has no meaning.
  - `? @TEXT("${name}", key="name") => @PLACE(1.5, 1.5)`: the text is attached to the object drawn with the symbol, and `${…}` reads its attributes. This PLACE is in local coordinates and transforms with the instance (see the direction of texts in 9.2.5). Without PLACE, the implementation puts the text next to the object.
  - A text with a key is a sub-object of the object drawn with the symbol, such as `#M1::name`. The texts in the symbol come first, and a text with the same key in the scene overrides it parameter by parameter: `#R1 @TEXT("R_{D}", key="name")` changes only the content and keeps the position from the symbol; `#M1::name @PLACE(13, 18)` changes only the position and keeps the content from the symbol. A position given in the scene is in scene coordinates and no longer turns with the instance.
  - The keys that a symbol gives exist only once symbols are assigned (8.2 steps 4 and 7). When the symbol of M1 has no text with the key `name` and the scene writes none, `#M1::name` fails. To be independent of the symbol, write a TEXT with that key in the scene.
  - A text attached to a point in the symbol is fixed text, such as `(-2, 2) @TEXT("+")` in an op-amp symbol, and cannot be changed for one instance.
- **Rules**: a rule such as `# @SELECT-INSTANCE => @SELECT-PIN(wired=false) => @TEXT(…)` selects pins, which come from the symbols, so it is evaluated in step 6 with the one-by-one generation (8.2). When an implementation adds flight lines or labels by itself, it skips pins that already have a label.
- Which net names are shown and where, and whether connections without wires are shown with labels or flight lines, are decided by the implementation by default: they are part of its house style (Principles 2.7). Showing labels instead of wires changes the layout, so the solver and the renderer use the same setting. RNL writes these only when needed: to state an intent (without positions), or to keep positions already decided.
- There are no positions along a wire: the coordinates of a wire are not fixed themselves.
- **`fix_rotate`**: a truth value, `false` by default. With `true`, the `rotate` of the text is taken in the scene: the characters keep that direction, whatever the orientation of the object the text is attached to. Its anchor still follows the object, and so does the side of the anchor on which the text box lies. An alignment says where the box lies from the anchor: to the right with `start`, to the left with `end`, above with `bottom` and `baseline`, below with `top`, and to neither side with `middle`. This direction is transformed with the orientation of the object, and the text is aligned as the result says. So a name written above a symbol, with `halign="middle"` and `valign="bottom"`, lies to the left of its anchor, aligned `end` and `middle`, when the symbol is turned by `"R90"`, and below it, aligned `top`, at `"MX"`; it reads horizontally in every orientation and stays outside the symbol. A box that stays above its anchor keeps the `bottom` or `baseline` it was written with.
- **`upright`**: a truth value, `true` by default: the text is turned by 180 degrees when it would otherwise read upside down, and its glyphs are not mirrored. With `false`, its characters follow the rotation and mirroring as they are (9.2.5).
- Parameters: `text` (required; may be given by position), `key`, `dx`, `dy`, `halign`, `valign`, `rotate`, `fix_rotate`, `upright`, and the text style properties `textcolor`, `size`, `font`, `bold`, `italic`, `textvisible` (9.3); a text attached to a point has no `key`. For `halign`, `valign` and `rotate`, see 9.2.5. The style parameters are the direct style of the text (8.3); how a text gets its style is in 11.3.

### 13.4 Annotations: graphics in the scene

```rnl
(50, 18) @TEXT("5T OTA testbench") => @SET-STYLE $note => %n1;
%f1 = @RECTANGLE {(0, 0) (40, 30)};
%n1 @PLACE(60, 18)                            /* moves the text; may also be written in another record */
```

- LINE, RECTANGLE, CIRCLE, ELLIPSE, ARC, BEZIER and TEXT can be written directly in the scene, and so also in the body of a general definition. In a symbol body their coordinates are local; in the scene they are scene coordinates. Where they are written decides this; the keyword is the same.
- The note objects of Principles 2.2 (free-form text, graphics, frames and other objects that are only drawn, with no terminals and no electrical meaning) are the annotations of this standard: graphics written in the scene, TEXTs attached to points, and the symbols drawn by ANNOTATION (13.5).
- PIN can be written only in the body of DEFINE-SYMBOL: a pin binds a terminal of the object drawn with the symbol, and the scene has no such object.
- A TEXT attached to a point is an annotation (13.3): it can refer only to the attributes `x` and `y` of the point, and it takes no `key=`, since a point has no sub-objects (with a key, the TEXT fails). To show the name or value of an object, attach the TEXT to the object, which makes it a label.
- The position of a graphic is given by its points. A point can be any point object: a named point, a free point with constraints, or `#M1::position`, which follows the device. In the body of a general definition, a literal point is an offset from the usage's own point (9.4). PLACE does not take a graphic itself: to move, from another record, a RECTANGLE, CIRCLE or ELLIPSE written with a centre, move its `::position`; LINE, ARC, BEZIER and the graphics written with two points have several points, so name the points and move them. TEXT is the exception: it has a position of its own, the point it is attached to only gives its default place, and PLACE sets the position of the text directly (13.3).
- Appearance: graphics can take style parameters directly (9.2.5) and also take SET-STYLE, with the two tiers of 8.3; a property given by neither has the default of the renderer.
- A frame is a RECTANGLE, or a LINE whose last point is its first. There is no graphic that encloses a group of objects: draw a RECTANGLE and constrain its corners to lie outside the group, such as `%tl %stage1_set @DISTANCE("left-to-right", min=1)`, which keeps the corner at least 1 to the left of every member (14.5); what is enclosed is the points of the members plus the margin. A viewer may draw a GROUP as a frame; that is up to the viewer (11.5). There is no `::box`.
- The drawing order follows layers and positions (11.6).
- **Class**: whether something is an annotation follows from its kind of object: graphics in the scene are annotations, so a renderer can show or hide them as a class. SELECT-ANNOTATION selects all graphics in the scene and what ANNOTATION draws (13.5), including those expanded from the bodies of general definitions (such as frames drawn by a house-style package); graphics in symbols, labels, wires and usages (including junctions) are not annotations. Finer classes (explanations, title blocks, decorations drawn by a package) are named sets, and a graphic can be in several sets. Showing and hiding is up to the viewer; the language has no `visible`.
- A text on a device is a TEXT attached to the device, which is a label (13.3). A TEXT attached to `#M1::position` is an annotation; by default it also follows the device, but it can refer only to the coordinates of that point, and it has no key.

### 13.5 Symbols drawn as annotations: ANNOTATION

```rnl
%a1 = (40, 20) @ANNOTATION(rotate=30) $ARROW_SYMBOL;     /* an arrow at any angle */
#M1 @ANNOTATION(rotate=-45, mirror=true) $ARROW_SYMBOL;   /* follows M1 */
%a1::position @PLACE(44, 20)                             /* moves the first one */
```

- ANNOTATION gathers the graphics of what it is given and draws them as one annotation (13.4), turned by any angle. Two slots: `at`, written first, one locatable object: a point, or an object whose position the annotation follows; and `source`, written last, one of:
  - a symbol (DEFINE-SYMBOL): the graphics and the TEXTs of its body;
  - a general definition (DEFINE), with roles or without: the graphics written directly in its body all of whose points are literal points, which are offsets from the point of a usage (9.4), and the TEXTs attached to such points or to `?`. The definition is not applied: no usage is made, so nothing is drawn twice;
  - a usage: the graphics of its symbol, when it has one, once the symbols are given (8.2), and the graphics that its body drew with literal points only, as for a definition, about the point of the usage. The usage itself stays drawn where it is.
- Everything else is left out: PINs, `box=`, constraints, the usages made in a body, and the graphics of a body that have a point on another object, such as a line drawn to a role, which would mean nothing once moved and turned. A graphic left out for that reason gives a hint; the rest is left out without one, so a symbol with pins can be reused for a sign. Nothing fills a role; the annotation has no pins, cannot be an end of a wire, and is in no tree of 13.7.
- **Parameters**: `rotate`, an angle in degrees, counterclockwise, any number, `0` by default; `mirror`, a truth value, `false` by default, which mirrors the symbol about its vertical axis before it is turned; and the style properties, given to its graphics and texts as SET-STYLE gives them (11.3). It also takes, by name, the parameters of a symbol or a general definition given as `source`, as AS does (9.4), such as `(0, 0) @ANNOTATION(rotate=30, w=5) $D`: a parameter not given takes its default, and one with no default counts as not written (9.4). Where a parameter of the source has the name of a parameter of ANNOTATION itself, such as `rotate` or `mirror`, the parameter of ANNOTATION is meant, and a hint is given. A usage given as `source` keeps the values of its own parameters, and a parameter of its definition written to ANNOTATION is then an invalid parameter. The texts of the symbol are turned with it as the texts of a symbol are turned with an instance, by their own `rotate`, `fix_rotate` and `upright` (9.2.5).
- In the texts of the symbol, `?` is the annotation, which has only the attributes `x` and `y`, as a point (13.4); another attribute reference there is drawn as the empty text.
- **Result**: a new annotation, with the sub-object `::position`, its anchor. Given a written point, the anchor starts there and can be moved by its `::position`, as a graphic written with a centre (13.4); given an object, it follows the position of the object. SELECT-ANNOTATION selects it, SET-STYLE styles it, and `textvisible` hides its texts.
- What it is is known where it is written: anything other than a symbol, a general definition or a usage in the slot `source` is an error; a source with no graphic to draw gives a warning and draws nothing.
- **Compared with the rest**: the graphics written in the scene (13.4) each have their own points; a usage with a symbol, such as a figure of the standard package, is drawn in the eight orientations of 11.2, may have pins and can be wired. ANNOTATION draws a symbol at any angle and has no electrical meaning.

### 13.6 Text markup and attribute references

```rnl
#R1 @TEXT("R_{D}", key="name");                              /* subscript */
(50, 18) @TEXT("\textbf{5T OTA} testbench\\VDD = 1.8 V");    /* bold, two lines */
#M1 @TEXT("W/L=${w}/${l}", key="wl");                        /* host parameters */
$NMOS_M = @DEFINE-SYMBOL {
  $NMOS;
  ? @TEXT("${model}", key="model") => @PLACE(1.5, -1.5)      /* the model name, in a symbol */
}
```

- **Markup** is a subset of the text mode of LaTeX, not full TeX:

  | Markup | Meaning |
  |---|---|
  | `_{…}`, `^{…}` | Subscript, superscript |
  | `\textbf{…}`, `\textit{…}` | Bold, italic |
  | `\overline{…}` | Overline, such as the active-low `\overline{RST}` |
  | `\subrest{…}` | The first character as written, the rest as a subscript: `\subrest{${name}}` shows M1 as M₁ and VDD as V with DD below |
  | `\subdigits{…}` | The digits at the end as a subscript: R12 as R₁₂, Vout1 as Vout₁; content without digits at the end is shown as it is |
  | `\(…\)` | Math mode (below) |
  | `\frac{…}{…}` | Fraction: numerator above, denominator below, a bar between, such as `\frac{${w}}{${l}}`; a viewer that cannot stack them shows "numerator/denominator" |
  | `\alpha` … `\omega`, `\Gamma` … `\Omega` | Greek letters, with the names of LaTeX |
  | `\pm`, `\times`, `\cdot`, `\infty`, `\approx`, `\neq`, `\leq`, `\geq`, `\ll`, `\gg`, `\propto`, `\parallel`, `\angle`, `\circ`, `\to`, `\uparrow`, `\downarrow` | Common symbols |
  | `\\` | Line break |
  | `\_`, `\{`, `\}`, `\$`, `\%`, `\&`, `\#`, `\textbackslash` | The characters `_`, `{`, `}`, `$`, `%`, `&`, `#`, `\` themselves |

- Greek letters: lower case `\alpha` to `\omega` (LaTeX has no `\omicron`; write o), and the variants `\varepsilon`, `\vartheta`, `\varpi`, `\varrho`, `\varsigma`, `\varphi`; upper case only the letters that differ from Latin ones: `\Gamma`, `\Delta`, `\Theta`, `\Lambda`, `\Xi`, `\Pi`, `\Sigma`, `\Upsilon`, `\Phi`, `\Psi`, `\Omega`. A symbol not in the table is written directly as a Unicode character (strings may hold non-ASCII characters, 3.7).
- Markup can be nested, such as `\textit{V}_{\textit{DD}}` and `I_{D_{1}}`. How the scripts of `V_{in}^{+}` are set (stacked or one after the other) is up to the viewer.
- **Subscripts and superscripts need braces**: a lone `_` or `^` is an ordinary character. This differs from LaTeX, where the m of `g_m` is a subscript: names and descriptions often contain `vin_1` or `bias_ctrl`, which that rule would spoil. In return, `g_m` is shown as written.
- Other characters are shown as written, including a lone `{`, `}`, `$`, `~`, `%`, `&` or `#`, which have other meanings in LaTeX.
- **Backslash**: `\\` is a line break, as in LaTeX; a backslash itself is written `\textbackslash`. A backslash that is not followed by a known command is shown as written and gives a warning, such as `C:\temp` (`\temp` is an unknown command; see the next item); to avoid the warning, write `C:\textbackslash{}temp`. `\textbackslash` is needed only where the backslash is directly followed by `\`, `{`, `}`, `_`, `^`, `$`, `%`, `&`, `#` or the name of a known command. RNL strings have no backslash escapes (3.7): `"\textbf{V}"` is written just so, not as `\\textbf`.
- An unknown command (`\` followed by letters that are not in the table, such as `\sqrt` or `\alpah`) and unbalanced braces are shown as written and give a warning; the text remains valid.
- **After a command name**: a command name is the run of letters directly after `\`. Spaces after the name of a known command are dropped and not shown, and an empty `{}` right after it only separates and is not shown either. So `10 \mu A` and `10 \mu{}A` both show 10 μA; to keep the space, write `\mu{} A`; `\textbackslash{}temp` shows \temp. Spaces after the escaped characters such as `\_` and `\{` are kept.

  Note: support for more of TeX in a later version only allows more. Math mode is written `\(…\)`, not `$…$`, since `$` is used by attribute references.
- **Formats of names**: `\subrest{…}` and `\subdigits{…}` change only how their content is shown: the characters shown are split into a normal part and a subscript. The content may be referenced, such as `\subrest{${name}}`; it is still not read as markup (below). They can be nested in other markup, such as `\textit{\subrest{${name}}}`.
- **Math mode**: `\(…\)` holds a formula, in a subset of the math mode of LaTeX:
  - letters are variables, set in italic; digits and the symbols of the table are upright, and spaces are not shown;
  - `_` and `^` take one character or a group in braces, as in LaTeX, so `g_m` is g with a subscript m inside `\(…\)`;
  - `\frac{…}{…}`, `\sqrt{…}`, the Greek letters and common symbols of the table, and the relations `=`, `<`, `>`;
  - `\mathrm{…}` sets its content upright, as for units and names, such as `\(V_\mathrm{DD}\)`;
  - an attribute reference inside is shown as written, upright.

  A command not in this subset is shown as written and gives a warning.
- **Fractions**: `\frac` takes two groups in braces directly after it; when one is missing, it is unknown markup, shown as written with a warning. Numerator and denominator may contain markup and `${…}`.
- How far scripts and fractions are raised or lowered and how much they are made smaller is up to the viewer. The font, and bold and italic for the whole text, come from the style or from the parameters `font`, `bold`, `italic` (9.3).
- Markup is read in the `text` of TEXT and in the `label` of PIN (such as `\overline{CS}`). Other string parameters (`key`, enumeration values, selection conditions) do not read markup.
- Note: in import and export, the `_{…}` and `^{…}` of KiCad are the same, and its `~{…}` is `\overline{…}`; an overline that Altium writes with `\` after each character is rewritten as `\overline{…}`.
- **Attribute references** `${name}` show an attribute of the object the text is attached to. To write `${` itself, write `\${`; everything from `\${` to its matching `}` is shown as written, and that `}` does not count as an unbalanced brace. The attributes are:
  - Instances: `name`; `value`, the value written on the host line, as written, empty if there is none (it does not fall back to the model name); `model` and `type`, as in selection conditions (10.1), so that `${model}` of an X instance shows the subcircuit name; and the host parameters, such as `w`. A host parameter with the name of a standard attribute is written `${#…}` (see below).
  - Terminals, pins and wires: `net`, the net they are on, shown by its name; for a pin, the net of `::net` (6.5), and a pin that binds nothing has no `net`. On the empty node (6.3), `net` is the empty text, and so is a role filled with it, such as `${p}`: nothing is drawn (13.3). The `net` of a bus wire is the names of its nets in order, joined with `,` and no spaces (13.1). For a terminal of a bundle, it is the name of the net when all members are on the same net, and otherwise the names joined with `,` (11.8).
  - Nets: `name`.
  - Usages: the roles and the parameters of their definition. `${p}` is the object that fills the role p, shown by the rules below. A usage has no name of its own: the symbols of grounds, supplies and ports write `${p}`, which shows the name of the net when p is a net; a role filled with a terminal or a pin, such as t of `$GROUND_STUB`, shows the name of its net too.
  - Bundles: an attribute that is the same for all members has that value; otherwise the values are joined with `,` (11.8).
  - Points: `x`, `y`, the final coordinates, that is, after solving.
  - Graphics: no attributes.
- **Attribute paths**: in `${a::b::c}`, the first name takes an attribute of the attached object, and each following `::name` takes an attribute of the value before it, such as `${p::x}` (the x of the role p when it is a point) and `${pair::left::w}`.
  - The value of an attribute can be an object (a role of a usage; the `net` of a terminal or wire) or text (`name`, `value`, a host parameter and so on). Text has no attributes, so no `::` may follow it.
  - A value on the path can also be a set, such as a set role. The rest of the path is then taken from each of its members, a set among them by its own members, in the order of the set, and the values are shown as a set is (below); when one member lacks the attribute, the text fails. So for a usage whose set role `pairs` holds two differential pairs, `${pairs::left::w}` shows `w` for both `left` transistors, such as `1u,2u`.
  - In `${…}`, `::` takes attributes only, unlike `::` in statements, which takes sub-objects (6.5): `${m::name}` is the name of the instance m, while `#M1::name` in a statement is the text with the key `name` on M1 (13.3).
  - `${m::#type}` looks only for a host parameter, as `${#type}` does (see below).
- **Display**: when the last value of the path is text, it is shown as written. When it is an object: instances and nets show their names; terminals, pins and wires show the name of their net, and a bus wire the names of its nets joined with `,`; bundles as described above; a set (such as a set role) shows its members, each in this way, joined with `,`. Points, usages and graphics cannot be shown: the text fails. So when the role p is a point, write `${p::x}`, not `${p}`.
- References give the text as written in the netlist, without evaluating it: `w=1u` shows 1u. What each dialect provides is defined by its host adapter; in Spectre, for example, the value of a resistor is the parameter `r`.
- The attributes listed above always exist; one that has no value is empty. A reference to an attribute name that does not exist makes the text fail.
- The referenced content is shown as written and is not read as markup: host names and values are not written in this markup.
- References go only down from the attributes of the attached object; they cannot reach an arbitrary object. There is no reference to another object such as `${R3:VALUE}` of KiCad.
- **Aliases**: attribute names in `${…}` also accept the aliases declared for the file, looked up as standard attribute first, then alias, then host parameter; `${#name}` looks only for a host parameter (16.5). The role and parameter names of a usage are RNL names: they accept only the `definition=` aliases of its definition, looked up at each step in the definition of the usage of that step, such as `${输入对::左管::w}` (16.5).
- **Host parameters with standard names**: a standard attribute comes first, and a host parameter with the same name is written `${#type}` (`#` stands for the host in RNL). Any host parameter can be written this way; `${w}` is short for `${#w}` where no standard attribute and no alias has that name. Standard attribute names are compared exactly as written, host parameter names by the rules of the host: in a SPICE host, `${Type}` is not a standard attribute, so it is the host parameter type.
- **Formatting names**: referenced content is formatted only as a whole, with markup around it, such as `\textit{${name}}`, and by the two commands `\subrest{…}` and `\subdigits{…}`, which show the rest of a name, or its digits at the end, as a subscript. Other formats of single characters are written as text, such as `#M1 @TEXT("M_{1}", key="name")`, which does not follow a renaming; a further convention of a viewer is its house style.

### 13.7 Drawing connections as wires: ROUTE-NET, ROUTE-OBJECT, UNROUTE-NET, UNROUTE-OBJECT

```rnl
&x @ROUTE-NET;                                /* x is drawn as wires; the solver decides the branches and paths */
%jo = (_, _);
&out @WIRE {#M2:1 %jo};
&out @ROUTE-NET;                              /* this wire is drawn as written; the solver adds the rest */
{#M1 #M2 #M5} @ROUTE-OBJECT;                  /* on each net that two or more of their pins share, those pins are joined */
&vdd @UNROUTE-NET(priority="high");           /* the pins of vdd are not joined, whatever asks for it at a lower priority */
# @SELECT-NET(name="clk*", recursive=true) => @ROUTE-NET   /* as a house-style package writes it, with nets chosen by a rule */
```

- These keywords say which connections are drawn as wires, without saying where they branch and where the dots go; the solver decides that.
- **ROUTE-NET** joins the pins of a net into a tree: the pins of the devices on the net, and the pins of virtual parts whose `::net` is the net, that is, the pins bound to roles filled with it (12.1). One slot, which takes nets; a set is taken member by member (5.2.1), and is not a bus.
- **ROUTE-OBJECT** joins the pins of some objects: for each net on which two or more of their pins lie, those pins are joined into a tree. One slot, which takes a set of objects; it passes its input on. An instance or a bundle stands for its pins, a terminal for its pin, and a usage for the objects that fill its roles, down to the lowest objects, as for the members of a group (11.5); a usage with a symbol stands for its own pins as well, each on the net of the role it binds (12.1). Objects without pins, such as a net or a point that fills a role, add none: a whole net is drawn with ROUTE-NET. So a definition of the standard package can draw the connections inside its structure, such as `{?left ?right ?tail} @ROUTE-OBJECT(priority="low")`, without joining the rest of the nets.
- **UNROUTE-NET** and **UNROUTE-OBJECT** take pins out: the pins of a net, or of some objects (counted as for ROUTE-OBJECT), are not joined by the trees of ROUTE-NET and ROUTE-OBJECT.
- **Each pin and each tree on its own**: a pin covered by a ROUTE-NET or ROUTE-OBJECT is in the tree of that statement unless an UNROUTE-NET or UNROUTE-OBJECT that covers the pin wins over that statement: it has a higher priority (14.8), or the same priority and is written later, in the order of positions of 8.5. The comparison is made with each ROUTE statement apart, so a pin can be taken out of the tree of a ROUTE-OBJECT of priority `"low"` by an UNROUTE of priority `"normal"` and stay in the tree of a ROUTE-NET of priority `"high"`. A statement with `"disabled"` is left out. These statements are not geometric facts and take no part in conflicts (8.5); they decide only which pins the solver joins.
- **When**: the statements of these keywords take effect from step 3 of 8.2. Their rules, such as the last line of the example, are evaluated in step 5, before generation: their conditions read the trees of the statements, and generation, such as the fill of the standard package, sees the trees the rules make (10.2, 10.4).
- **Wires written are not affected**: an explicit WIRE is drawn as written, also on a net or at a pin taken out by UNROUTE. Wires already written are part of the trees they touch, and the solver adds the rest.
- **Trees**: each ROUTE-NET makes one tree on its net, and each ROUTE-OBJECT one tree on each net it joins. Trees on one net that share a pin, or that written wires join, are one tree; it takes the style of the statement first in the order of positions, and a hint is given when their styles differ. A net may have several trees that nothing joins, such as two ROUTE-OBJECT on devices far apart.
- Only pins are joined: terminals without a pin are in no tree (9.2.4). A pin on the empty node is on no net (6.3), so ROUTE-OBJECT does not join it; it is connected only by a wire written on the empty node (13.1). A tree with fewer than two points to connect does nothing. Whether a pin is wired (10.2) is decided before generation, from the trees without the virtual parts that generation adds: a net that has one pin of its own, such as an input that drives one gate, makes a tree of one point, so that pin is not wired and the fill gives it what it gives a pin without a wire, even when the net is routed to the port of its level. To route such a net to its port, write the port tag in the file, such as `$IO_PORT @ATTACH &x` or `$IO_PORT_STUB` of the standard package (12.4, 16.3): a port tag that the fill adds does not count.
- **Parameters**: ROUTE-NET and ROUTE-OBJECT take `mode` (a hint for the solver, with the values of WIRE, default `"auto"`), the style parameters of wires (13.1) and `priority`; UNROUTE-NET and UNROUTE-OBJECT take `priority`.
- Note: a WIRE with an empty list of points does not mean ROUTE-NET. A list of points may come from a set that happens to be empty, and an empty list would then silently connect everything; so these are keywords of their own; this version has no optional slots either (5.3).
- **Written back by the solver**: concrete WIREs (with their bends and `mode="straight"`), the named points where they branch, and the dots, when `junction` is set (13.2), with the style parameters of the tree, go into the solver's own record. The ROUTE-NET and ROUTE-OBJECT statements are the intent of the user and stay; the next solve computes them again (chapter 17). To branch from the middle of a wire written by the author, the solver splits that wire (13.1).
- **Viewers that do not solve** draw flight lines or nothing, as the viewer decides (Principles 2.7).
- A house-style package chooses which nets are drawn as wires with sets and rules, as in the example; there is no keyword for how a net is shown.
