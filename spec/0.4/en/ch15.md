## 15 Electrical semantics

### 15.1 Applying semantic definitions

```rnl
%dp1 = #M1(left) #M2(right) #M5(tail) @AS $DIFFERENTIAL_PAIR_N;
%cm1 = {#M3 #M9}(ref) {#M4 #M6}(out) @AS $CURRENT_MIRROR_P;
%in = {#M8 #M5} @AS $MATCHED
```

- What objects mean is marked by applying a semantic definition with AS (9.5): the application makes a usage, and the objects fill the roles of the role list of the definition. Marking a meaning and stating a layout relation are the same kind of statement.
- A usage can be a member of another usage (nesting), as in the examples of 9.4 and 10.1.
- A set role, written `"{name}"` in the role list (such as `"{members}"` of `$MATCHED`, and `"{ref}"` and `"{out}"` of `$CURRENT_MIRROR_P` in the example), takes one set, for a number of members that is not fixed (9.4).
- A role can declare the kind of object it takes, such as `roles=[["in_p", "instance"], ["in_n", "instance"]]`; an object of the wrong kind makes the whole application fail (9.4). A definition with an empty body checks nothing of what fills the roles, so without such declarations a wrong object cannot be detected there.
- The role names of a usage are its sub-objects, such as `%dp1::left`.
- Applying a definition expands its body, and the body may contain constraints, such as a default drawing at priority `"low"`, which the priority of the usage turns off (9.5, 14.8).
- Not checked: whether the meaning agrees with the connections, types or parameters of the netlist is not checked (Principles 2.4). For example, the language does not check for `#M3(ref) #M4(out) @AS $MY_MIRROR` whether the gate and the drain of M3 are connected; a definition checks what it claims with the assertions of its body (15.3, 15.4).
- The usages of a given meaning are selected with SELECT-USAGE, and the usages that contain a given object with SELECT-ANCESTOR-USAGE; the members of usages are reached with a selection whose scope is the usages (10.1).
- The vocabulary of semantic definitions belongs to the standard package (9.5), which provides definitions such as `$DIFFERENTIAL_PAIR_N`.
- The basic validity of one description (Principles 2.4) is: its references can be found, its roles receive objects of the right kinds, its single roles are filled, and each statement it expands to is valid itself (9.5). Whether two roles are filled with the same object is not checked.

### 15.2 Current direction: CURRENT

```rnl
#M5:1 @CURRENT("in");                 /* drain: the current flows in */
#M5:3 @CURRENT("out")                 /* source: the current flows out */
```

- CURRENT sets the direction of the static current at a terminal: `"in"` (it flows in), `"out"` (it flows out) or `"unspecified"` (used to override a value given by a rule of a package). The parameter is `direction`. A CURRENT rule is evaluated in step 6 (10.4). A CURRENT whose object is a pin, written so or reaching it through a role, needs the symbol to find its terminal (5.2.4): it is not a rule, and it takes effect once the symbols of its object are given, in step 4, or in step 7 for an object generated in step 6. Up to step 6, the `current` condition of selections reads only the CURRENT statements written on terminals; from step 7 on, as in the style queue, it reads all directions, and so do the geometry and the check below.
- It is a fact about the terminal, not a geometric fact: it takes no part in conflicts (8.5), and a solver uses it only as a hint, such as for the order in which it tries orientations. Like ORIENT, it is a modifying statement and passes on its input handle. It has a key, one per terminal, and the later one wins. A terminal for which it is never written is unspecified.
- It is given only on terminals. The current inside a device with several terminals cannot be split up clearly: for a three-terminal device, "a flows to b, b flows to c" leaves it unclear whether the current at b flows in or out, while the direction of the static current at a terminal always has a definite meaning.
- It takes only terminals of the host, including the ports of this level: `#:inp @CURRENT("in")` means that the current flows into the subcircuit there. A pin of a device converts to its terminal, such as `#M1::d @CURRENT("in")`; virtual parts have no terminals, so their pins do not convert (5.2.4). A terminal without a pin is allowed (unlike for wires).
- Not checked against the netlist (Principles 2.4). The CURRENT statements of one net together make an assertion (15.3): when every terminal on the net, the ports of this level included, has a CURRENT of `"in"` or `"out"`, and all of them move current the same way with respect to the net, it gives a violation (7.6) that names the net and its terminals, since the current has nowhere to go. A device terminal with `"in"` takes current out of the net, and a port of this level with `"in"` brings current into it. A terminal on the net without CURRENT, or with `"unspecified"`, leaves the net unchecked. A global net (6.3), such as `0`, is not checked either: it is the same node in every level, so the current may leave through the terminals of other levels. A CURRENT in a record, or in the body of a usage, whose priority is `"disabled"` does not count. It is checked with the assertions, after step 7 of 8.2, and like them it is only a report, which names the net, its terminals and their CURRENT statements; the CURRENT facts stay in effect.
- Use: the current at the terminal runs in the direction in which a wire leaves the pin (9.2.4), turned with the orientation of the symbol, when it flows out, and the other way when it flows in. A solver can use this to choose among the layouts, such as preferring the current to run downwards (14.8); up or down is a matter of house style. A terminal without CURRENT, with `"unspecified"`, or whose pin does not write `direction=` (9.2.4) gives no direction; a direction that an implementation infers from the graphics does not count here. Rules can select terminals by the direction of their current, with the condition `current` of SELECT-TERMINAL (10.1).
- **Arrows**: CURRENT draws nothing. Whether and how arrows are drawn from CURRENT is a preference of the viewer (part of its house style), like which net names are shown (13.3). A viewer that draws them itself skips terminals that already have a usage of the standard current arrow, as it recognizes the standard `$NO_CONNECT` (12.6). Arrows to be kept (from an imported schematic, or decided by the author) are written as explicit objects:

  ```rnl
  $CURRENT_ARROW @ATTACH #M5:1 => @ORIENT("R270") => @TEXT("I_{SS}", dx=1);   /* on the drain pin, with I_SS beside it */
  $CURRENT_ARROW @ATTACH (12, 20) => @ORIENT("R270")                           /* in the middle of a wire, at absolute coordinates */
  ```

  - An arrow drawn on a terminal is a usage whose definition ties its point to the pin of the terminal (12.1 item 5). It does not turn with the pin, so its orientation is written; the text beside it is attached to the usage (13.3).
  - An arrow in the middle of a wire is not anchored to the wire: it is drawn at a point, with absolute coordinates.
  - How an arrow pointing in is told from one pointing out, and how the definition and the symbol of the arrow are drawn, belong to the standard package. The symbol can be just a line with an arrowhead at its end (9.2.5).
  - Drawn arrows are kept apart from CURRENT: if they were one thing, exporting an arrow would change what the layout aims for.

### 15.3 Assertions: ASSERT-INSTANCE, ASSERT-TERMINAL, ASSERT-NET, ASSERT-USAGE, ASSERT-IN

```rnl
$MATCHED_PAIR = @DEFINE(roles=[["left", "instance"], ["right", "instance"]]) {
  {?left ?right} @ASSERT-INSTANCE(same=["model", "w", "l"], different=["name"]);
  {?left ?right} @SELECT-TERMINAL(terminal="s") => @ASSERT-TERMINAL(same=["net"], message="the sources of a pair share a net")
};
# @SELECT-INSTANCE(name="MD*") => @ASSERT-INSTANCE(type="nmos");
%dp1 @ASSERT-USAGE $DIFFERENTIAL_PAIR_N;                      /* %dp1 is a usage of this definition */
#M5:s @SELECT-NET => @ASSERT-IN $GROUND_NET_SET              /* the source of M5 is on a ground net */
```

- An assertion checks that what a file or a definition says agrees with the facts of the circuit, such as that the two halves of a pair have the same model and size, or that dummies are of one type. It only reports: it makes no fact, changes nothing in the drawing and takes no part in resolution (8.5).
- **One keyword for each kind**, as for selections (10.1): ASSERT-INSTANCE checks instances (and bundles, by their first member, 11.8), ASSERT-TERMINAL terminals, ASSERT-NET nets, ASSERT-USAGE usages. The one slot takes a set of objects of that kind; a single object is a set of one. The kind of an object is known when the text is read, and a wrong kind is an error there (5.2.4), so no assertion checks kinds.
- **Conditions** have the names and meanings of the conditions of the selection keyword of the same kind (10.1), such as `type="nmos"`, `terminals=4`, `net="vss"` or `port=true`: every member must meet each condition written.
- **`same=[…]`**: for each attribute in the list, all members have the same value. **`different=[…]`**: for each attribute in the list, any two members have different values. The attribute names are those of `${…}` (13.6): for instances `name`, `value`, `model`, `type` and the names of their parameters; for terminals `net`; for nets `name`; for usages `definition`. For terminals, nets and usages these are all: any other name is a wrong parameter value, and the assertion fails (7.1). For instances the names of parameters are open, so any name is taken, and an attribute that a member does not have counts as a value of its own, "absent": members that lack it are equal to one another and differ from every written value. So `same=["nf"]` holds when no member writes `nf`, and gives a violation when some write it and some do not; a process whose devices have no such parameter needs no assertion of its own. An instance without a type (10.1) lacks `type`. Values are compared as the host writes them, without evaluation (there are no expressions, 3.7).
- **`has=[…]`**, for ASSERT-INSTANCE: every member has each attribute in the list, such as `has=["w", "l"]`; a member that lacks one gives a violation. `same` does not ask that an attribute be written; `has` does.
- **ASSERT-USAGE** takes a definition as its last object, as SELECT-USAGE does (5.6, 10.1); it may be left out. With it, every member must be a usage of that definition, recognized by what its name resolves to (9.5), not by the spelling of the name. Its conditions are `symbol` and `drawn` (10.1). `same=["definition"]` holds when all members are usages of one definition, and `different=["definition"]` when no two are.
- **ASSERT-IN**: `X @ASSERT-IN S` checks that every member of X is a member of the set S, which is written last, as for ADD-TO (5.2.2); S must be a set and is not wrapped into one. Whether two members are the same is decided by handle (5.2). The members may be of any kind, and S may depend on selections (10.4). It has no conditions, only `message`, and a violation names the members of X that S does not hold. With a difference of sets (5.2.2), it also says that a set is empty.
- `different=["name"]` checks that instances or nets are different objects, as when two roles must not be filled with the same object; terminals have no `name`, so for them `name` is a wrong parameter value, in `different` as in `same`.
- **The report**: a check that does not hold gives a violation (7.6) that names the members and the attribute; **`message`** adds a text to it. Nothing fails, and the drawing is the same as without the assertion: an assertion is only a report, for the author and for tools such as `rnl check`.
- **Where and when**: in any level, and in the body of a general definition, where it is checked for each application (9.4). Assertions are checked after step 7 of 8.2, when every condition can be read, also `drawn` and `symbol` (10.1). A set with no members holds trivially, and so does an assertion given the empty node (6.3).
- An assertion has no priority; in a record with `priority="disabled"`, and in the body of a usage whose priority is `"disabled"` (14.8), it is not checked.

### 15.4 Topology assertions: ASSERT-TOPOLOGY

```rnl
{#R1 #R2 #R3} @ASSERT-TOPOLOGY("series");                  /* a chain: R1 to R2 to R3 */
{#M3 #M4} @ASSERT-TOPOLOGY("parallel", exclude=[["nmos", "g"], ["nmos", "b"]]);   /* drains on one net, sources on one net */
{#L1 #C1 #L2} @ASSERT-TOPOLOGY("star");                    /* a T network: three branches meet at one net */
{#C1 #L1 #C2} @ASSERT-TOPOLOGY("loop");                    /* a pi network: C1, L1 and C2 go round */
{%branch1 %branch2} @ASSERT-TOPOLOGY("parallel")           /* two branches, each a usage or a set taken as a black box, between the same nets */
```

- ASSERT-TOPOLOGY checks that some members are connected in a given way: in series, in parallel, as a star or as a single loop. It is an assertion like those of 15.3, and what 15.3 says of them holds for it too: it only reports, a check that does not hold gives a violation (7.6), to which `message` adds a text, and where, when and under which priority it is checked is the same.
- **The members**: the one slot takes a set of members, in order, counted by items (5.2): an object is one member, and an item that is a `{…}` or a reference to a set is one member, a black box. A set that is the only object is split one level first (5.2.1), so `%chain @ASSERT-TOPOLOGY("series")` checks the items of the chain, and `{%branch1 %branch2}` has two members. A member is thus an instance (or a bundle, by its first member, 11.8), a usage or a set. Given the result of a selection, which has one item for each member, the members are in the order of the result (10.3). With fewer than two members the check holds trivially.
- **The nets of a member**:
  - an instance: the nets on which lie those of its terminals that `exclude` does not take out;
  - a usage or a set: a black box, one element made of the terminals it brings. A usage, with a symbol or not, stands for what fills its roles, and a set for its members, followed inward down to the innermost; an instance brings all its terminals, a terminal itself only, a pin the terminals it binds, and nets and points bring nothing; a bundle counts by its first member. The nets of the black box are those on which lie the terminals it brings that `exclude` does not take out, and that are used outside it too: by a terminal it does not bring, those of the other members of a bundle included, or as a port of the level. A net used only by the terminals it brings does not count. Note: a net that two members share is used outside each of them by the other, so whether a net is a port changes a verdict only for members that bring the same terminal; otherwise it changes only which nets are the black box's, as a tool may show them.
- **`exclude=[[type, terminal], …]`**: terminals that do not count, as pairs of a type, compared as the condition `type` (10.1), and a terminal name or number, such as `exclude=[["nmos", "g"], ["nmos", "b"]]`. The terminal of a pair is compared as the condition `terminal` of SELECT-TERMINAL (10.1): a number by place, a name by the case rules of the host; a name that the instance does not have matches nothing. A terminal of an instance without a type, or of a type that the list does not name, counts. Without `exclude`, every terminal counts. A pair that is not an array of two strings, or a string and a whole number, is a wrong parameter value, and the assertion fails (7.1).
- **The topologies**, the first parameter, given by position, one of four:
  - `"series"`: each two members next to each other in the order share exactly one net, and two members not next to each other share none. What else is on a shared net is not checked: the middle of a divider may lead elsewhere.
  - `"parallel"`: at least two nets, the ends, are shared by all the members. Other nets of a member, such as a tap that a branch brings out, are not checked, as nothing else on a shared net is checked for `"series"`.
  - `"star"`: one net is shared by all the members, and no two members share any other net.
  - `"loop"`: at least three members. Going round in the order, the last and the first included, each two next to each other share exactly one net, these nets all differ, and two members not next to each other share none. With two members, `"loop"` gives a violation; write `"parallel"`.
