## 12 Virtual parts

### 12.1 Concept

- A virtual part is a part that exists only in RNL, such as a ground, a supply, a port tag or a no-connect mark. It does not exist electrically: it is only drawn, and it neither creates nor changes electrical facts.
- A virtual part is a usage that has a symbol; there is no separate keyword or kind of object for it. `$GROUND @ATTACH &vss` makes a usage; the standard package gives the usages of `$GROUND` a ground symbol, so it is drawn.
- Rules:
  1. **Pins and roles.** A usage has no terminals. The pins of its symbol are reached by name, such as `%g0::pin` (6.5), and `%g0::p` is still the member that fills role p, the net vss. A pin whose `terminal=` names a role of the definition, matched by name or by `terminals=` (11.3), is bound to that role: it is drawn on what fills the role, so a wire on that net may end there and ROUTE-NET joins it (13.7), and `::net` of the pin is that net. A pin that binds a role is drawn on the net of what fills it: a net, or the net of a terminal or a pin (5.2.4). So `$GROUND @ATTACH #M5:3` puts a ground on the net of M5:3, at a position of its own, not on that pin; to draw a ground at the pin, use a definition whose body does so, such as `$GROUND_STUB` (10.5). A bound role filled with the empty node (6.3) puts the pin on no net, which is valid: it is then joined only by wires written on the empty node (13.1), and the fill of the standard package gives it nothing. A bound role filled with another kind, such as an instance or a point, gives the pin no net: an error of kind, for which SET-SYMBOL fails for that usage, which is not drawn, and the diagnostic points to the application too. A role declared with the kind `"net"` converts a terminal to its net when the definition is applied (9.4), and a pin once the symbols are given (5.2.4). A role that no pin binds takes the kinds its definition declares, such as the terminal of `$NO_CONNECT` (12.6). It is not an electrical terminal: SELECT-TERMINAL does not select it and ASSERT-TERMINAL does not check it; SELECT-PIN does (10.1). A pin that binds no role is drawn only; it is wired by hand through its name. A pin name may not be the same as a role name (6.5).
  2. A usage is drawn only when it has been given a symbol; a usage without a symbol is not drawn. The standard package gives symbols only to the definitions that are meant to be drawn, so semantic usages such as differential pairs are not drawn.
  3. A usage without a symbol draws nothing itself: ORIENT on it fails, and so does a wire to a pin name of it, since it has no pins (9.2.4); this is checked after step 7 of 8.2. It still has its own point, `%u::position`: PLACE takes that point, and so do constraints written on `%u::position`, while a constraint on the usage itself takes its members (14.1); the point shows in the drawing only through what the body of its definition ties to it (14.1, 9.4). A wire or a graphic may end at that point only through `::position`, such as `&n {%u::position %pt} @WIRE`: it is a point, not a pin, so the wire connects nothing (5.2.4, 6.5). A style given to it is not an error: the style passes to the drawable objects created in its body (8.3), and has no visible effect when nothing is drawn in the body (11.3).
  4. A drawn usage draws only its own symbol. It neither contains nor moves its members, and it is not a visual container.
  5. **Where it is drawn.** The origin of its symbol is the usage's own point (14.1): PLACE and constraints move it, ORIENT turns the symbol about it (11.2), and it does not follow the objects that fill the roles, except through facts, such as wires to its pins and the facts in the body of its definition. A definition that draws a mark on a pin or at a point ties the point of the usage to it in its body, such as `{? ?t} @ALIGN("x"); {? ?t} @ALIGN("y")` (9.4). Without ORIENT, the solver chooses its orientation and writes it back (14.8), and a renderer that has no solution for it draws `"R0"` (8.5); it does not follow the direction in which wires leave a pin. The object that fills the first role is the main object of the usage; a usage has no name of its own, and texts refer to the main object through the role, such as `${p}` (13.6).
  6. The body of a definition is always expanded, so the definition of a virtual part may have a body.
- In terms of Principles 2.2, a virtual part is a usage with a symbol whose roles are filled with host objects; a standard virtual part is a definition of the standard package that comes with a rule giving it a default symbol; a user-defined virtual part is a definition of the user's own, together with a symbol rule or a symbol given in its body.
- **Defining a virtual part.** The standard way is a general definition with, written right after it, the rule that gives its usages their symbol: `$GROUND = @DEFINE(roles=[["p", "net"]]) {}; # @SELECT-USAGE(recursive=true) $GROUND => @SET-SYMBOL $GROUND_SYMBOL`. The rule takes its place in the symbol queue by position (8.3), so a later rule, of a house-style package or of the file, draws the same usages otherwise, such as `# @SELECT-USAGE(recursive=true) $GROUND => @SET-SYMBOL $EARTH`, also those made inside other definitions or by the fill; one usage is drawn otherwise by naming it, `%g1 @SET-SYMBOL $EARTH`. The body may give the symbol too, `? @SET-SYMBOL $X`, where the lone `?` is the usage (9.4): that names the usage directly (8.3), so no rule replaces it, which suits a part that is not meant to be drawn otherwise. Changing the drawing does not change the terminals or the wires on them.
- Whether a usage is a virtual part depends only on whether it has a symbol.
- Note: a definition that should be drawn but is given no symbol is reported only when one of its usages is oriented or wired, or when a constraint on it has no visible effect (14.1).

### 12.2 Creating virtual parts

```rnl id=g0
$GROUND @ATTACH &vss => @PLACE(24, 38) => %g0;
&vss @WIRE {#M5:3 %g0::pin}
```

- In `$D @ATTACH &net`, the objects after the definition are matched to the role list of the definition. It is written with ATTACH and read as "add this on the net" (9.4). A virtual part with several roles is written like an instance line of the netlist: for a definition `$DIFF_PORT` with the roles a and b, `$DIFF_PORT @ATTACH &inp(a) &inn(b)`, or by order, `$DIFF_PORT @ATTACH &inp &inn`.
- The roles are usually filled with nets: the pins of a virtual part that bind its roles are drawn on those nets (12.1 item 1), and its relation to a pin of a device (where it is drawn, what it connects to) is given by wires and constraints (12.3). A pin that binds a role is drawn on the net of what fills it (12.1 item 1): `$GROUND @ATTACH #M5:3` attaches a ground to the net of M5:3, as `$GROUND @ATTACH #M5:3::net` does, at a position of its own. The kinds of object a role takes can be declared in the definition (9.4), such as `roles=[["p", "net"]]`.
- This association is descriptive only: it makes no electrical connection and is not checked (Principles 2.2).
- A net can have any number of virtual parts, such as five grounds and two probes. Usages have no key: each application makes one more usage (5.8).
- A single virtual part can be given another symbol, such as `%g1 @SET-SYMBOL $EARTH` with a symbol `$EARTH` of one's own.
- ATTACH has no parameters of its own other than `priority` (14.8). The direction of a port belongs to the content of the standard package (12.4); the name shown is that of the main object, and to show another text, write a label (13.3). The parameters of the definition are passed through ATTACH as usual (9.4).
- `%g0` is bound only once in a level (6.6); to change its position, write `%g0 @PLACE(…)`.

### 12.3 Positions relative to a terminal

Parameters cannot hold host references, so a position relative to a terminal is written as constraints:

```rnl use=g0
{%g0::pin #M5:3} @ALIGN("x");
%g0::pin #M5:3 @DISTANCE("bottom-to-top", exact=2)    /* the pin of the ground 2 below the source of M5 */
```

With `@ALIGN("y")` in place of the DISTANCE, the pin of the ground lies on the pin of M5. A wire of length 0 between the two pins, `&vss @WIRE {#M5:3 %g0::pin}` (13.1), also makes the pin wired, so that the fill adds no second ground there (10.2, 10.5).

### 12.4 Ports

```rnl
$IO_PORT @ATTACH &inp => @PLACE(0, 20) => %p_inp
```

- The name of a port of a subcircuit is the name of the net inside it, so a port tag is associated with the net `&inp`.
- A port itself (`#:inp`) is not drawn; it is shown only by a virtual part. The fill of the standard series 0.4 gives every port net a port tag, supply and ground nets too, through the set `$PORT_FILL_SET`, leaving out the nets that have a port tag written (10.5); the tags of a level stand in one column, each at R0 with its pin to the right, and the column stays at least 6 units left of every instance of the level, measured from its own point, by constraints of priority `"low"`; the solver decides the rest. A file takes a net out of the set to leave it without a tag, such as `# @SELECT-NET(name="vdd") => @REMOVE-FROM $PORT_FILL_SET`; writes `$IO_PORT @ATTACH` for a tag it places itself; or writes `$PORT_LIST @ATTACH` once in a level for the same column, written out, and the fill then adds none in that level. The own point of a `$PORT_LIST` stands on its column (14.1), so a constraint on the usage moves the column.
- The direction of a port is only a matter of display: the netlist has no directions, and the user decides which symbol is drawn. Which port definitions and symbols there are belongs to the content of the standard package.
- **Orientation of port tags.** A port tag has a direction: the pin of `$IO_PORT_SYMBOL` in the standard package, for example, is at one end, and the tag extends the other way. Without ORIENT, the solver chooses the orientation from the directions of the wires, aiming at few bends. This is a goal of the solver, not part of the language (14.8), so the ORIENT of a port tag is usually not written by hand.

### 12.5 Standard virtual parts

The standard virtual parts belong to the standard package, which is not part of the language standard (16.3). The standard package provides, for example, a ground `$GROUND`, a supply `$SUPPLY`, a port tag `$IO_PORT` and a no-connect mark `$NO_CONNECT` (12.6), with their symbols, the rules that give these symbols, and the fill (10.5).

For one net vss, three ways of writing give three different drawings:

1. `&vss @ADD-TO $GROUND_NET_SET`: each terminal on vss without a wire gets a ground below it with a short wire (the fill of the standard package, 10.5). This is how textbooks usually draw it: a ground symbol under each grounded terminal.
2. `$GROUND @ATTACH &vss => %g`: one ground for the whole net, placed by the solver. The terminals are connected to its pin by wires, such as `&vss @WIRE {#M5:3 %g::pin}`, or by ROUTE-NET for the whole net (13.7).
3. `$GROUND_STUB @ATTACH #M5:3`: a ground with a short wire under the source of M5 only, as the fill draws it for each pin (10.5).

- A supply is written the same way, with the set `$SUPPLY_NET_SET` and the definition `$SUPPLY`. Net 0 is in `$GROUND_NET_SET` by default (10.1).
- For the third way, the wire in the body of the stub makes the pin wired, so the fill adds no second ground there (10.2, 10.5).
- Ports: the fill gives each port net of a subcircuit a port tag (12.4). To place one, constrain it or change the name it shows, write it out: `$IO_PORT @ATTACH &out => %pout`, with wires to `%pout::pin`, and `%pout @TEXT("V_{OUT}", key="name")` for the name (13.3). A net that should have no port tag is taken out of `$PORT_FILL_SET`.

### 12.6 No-connect marks

```rnl
$NO_CONNECT @ATTACH #M1:4    /* terminal 4 of M1 is left unconnected on purpose */
```

- A no-connect mark is the definition `$NO_CONNECT` of the standard package, whose role takes a terminal or a pin; there is no keyword for it. A terminal is taken as itself, not converted (5.2.4), so that a mark on one member of a bus pin (9.2.4), such as `$NO_CONNECT @ATTACH #U1:d<3>`, says which terminal is left open; several marks on one bus pin are drawn at that pin, one over another. No pin of the mark's symbol binds the role (12.1 item 1); the definition ties the point of the usage to the pin of what fills the role (12.1 item 5), and the standard package gives it a cross symbol with its origin at the centre of the cross and no pin.
- The usage records that the terminal is left unconnected on purpose: `# @SELECT-USAGE $NO_CONNECT => @SELECT-TERMINAL` selects these terminals.
- Checks (other host terminals on the net, a wire touching the terminal) are not required (Principles 2.4); an implementation that knows the standard `$NO_CONNECT` may give a warning.
- A no-connect mark does not count as a wire (10.2): a pin that has only the mark is still `wired=false`. For a fill by pin to skip these pins, subtract their pins, `# @SELECT-USAGE $NO_CONNECT => @SELECT-TERMINAL => @SELECT-PIN`, as in the difference of 10.5.
- Note: a no-connect mark is not a keyword because it has no use for an implementation other than drawing: the solver does not read it, and how it is drawn can be written in a definition and a symbol (1.5 item 7).
- Net labels are not virtual parts; see 13.3.
